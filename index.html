Democratic Participation in Digital Public History

Who owns memory in the digital age?

Who is remembered? Whose voices become visible? Who has the authority to interpret the past?

Who Owns Memory? is an international participatory public-history project exploring how difficult, marginalised and contested pasts are researched, interpreted and shared in the digital age. Through workshops, historical sources, oral testimony, photographs, archives, maps and digital storytelling, the project examines not only what we remember, but how memory is created, communicated, contested and made visible.

Five themes into the past

Projects are grouped thematically, but the categories remain deliberately porous. A story may connect migration with place, family memory with archives, or digital representation with difficult histories.

Exhibition theme

War & Difficult Pasts

Stories of war, persecution, genocide, repression, conflict and the contested remembrance of violent pasts.

Exhibition theme

Migration & Belonging

Histories of migration, forced displacement, exile, diaspora, borders, minority experience, identity and belonging.

Exhibition theme

Place & Local Memory

Projects that read landscapes, neighbourhoods, monuments, museums and historic sites as evidence of remembered and absent pasts.

Exhibition theme

Testimony & Family Memory

Stories built through oral history, family memory, photographs, personal collections and community archives.

Exhibition theme

Digital Memory & Democracy

Projects examining how history is created, circulated, contested and distorted through digital platforms, archives, AI and media.
Public-history practice

Question → evidence → interpretation → public

Do not begin with a tool. Begin with a focused question, audience, evidence and ethical plan.

The project treats digital public history as a method of historical inquiry and public engagement. Sources are contextualised instead of used as decoration, testimony is approached critically and ethically; absent voices are acknowledged instead of invented, and digital form is chosen because it serves the story.
