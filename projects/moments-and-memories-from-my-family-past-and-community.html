01

A family on the move

After the Second World War, Greece entered a civil war that lasted from 1946 to 1949. For my family, these years brought displacement, difficult work and profound loss.

My grandfather Christos left our family home and the village coffeehouse, which had been built in 1931 in Dipotamos, Kilkis. The family moved to the Serres region, including Vrontou, where they remained until the end of the Civil War in 1949.

My grandfather also took responsibility for the children of his younger brother, Alexis. Alexis and his eldest son had been sent into exile on the island of Gyaros. Both mothers had died: my grandmother Anatoli from illness, and my great-aunt, according to our family account, at the hands of National Army forces.

These experiences belong not only to the history of a war but also to the histories of ordinary families trying to survive. People left their homes in search of work and the possibility of a better life, often relocating to other parts of Greece.

02

Working to survive

Work was central to the family’s survival. My grandfather, my father and other relatives worked together on public projects in the Serres area.

The working days were long, often extending from dawn until sunset. People worked for more than eight hours, sometimes perhaps ten, with breaks in between.

When my aunt looks at the family photographs, she recognizes relatives engaged in public work: planting pine trees and cypresses along roadsides and drainage ditches. In the first photograph, she identifies her father Christos, who was a foreman; her brother, my father Konstantinos; and their cousin Anastasis among the people pictured.

The photographs reveal both a shared working environment and the personal consequences of displacement. The need to earn a living, together with political persecution, had brought family members far from their home.

03

A moment of joy

Even during difficult years, people found moments of connection, shared meals and celebration.

In the second photograph, my grandfather is seated at the centre of the table. He raises his glass in a cheerful toast with two other men. The photograph suggests a pause from work, a moment when they could share their worries and their joys.

They drank beer and listened to music. At the right of the photograph is what appears to be a gramophone, perhaps playing the songs of the time. The men's expressions are joyful; the image itself does not reveal the hardships of their everyday lives.

“Their faces appear happy, revealing nothing of the hardships of their daily lives.”From Eirini Anastasiadou’s family narrative

Looking at the scene today, I am struck by the sense of community and cooperation: people working side by side and finding ways to enjoy one another’s company. It invites me to reflect on what has changed in everyday social life since then.

04

Youth, bicycles and possibility

A third photograph preserves a more carefree moment from my family's past.

My father Konstantinos, about seventeen years old, is riding a bicycle beside his first cousin Anastasis. In the background stands his younger sister, my aunt Despina, who was around twelve at the time.

She watches them from a distance. Perhaps she wonders whether she, too, will be allowed to ride a bicycle. Perhaps she is waiting for her turn, or perhaps the family's financial circumstances meant there was no bicycle for her.

I also wonder whether the expectations placed on girls and women at the time shaped what was possible for her. The photograph cannot answer these questions. It gives us a reason to ask them.

05

Remembering together

My aunt Despina, born in 1937, remembers the years of displacement and the family’s time in Serres from a perspective different from that of the adults who worked.

In an interview recorded at her home on 25 September 2026, she reflected on the people and events shown in our family photographs. Looking at them, she recognized her father, brother and cousin, and recalled their daily work.

She also remembers those years with affection. As a child, she was the one member of the family who did not have to work; everyone else looked after her. Her memories of being cared for sit alongside the family's experiences of displacement, labour and bereavement.

These images are more than illustrations of a historical period. They are a way to ask questions across generations and to reconnect names, places and relationships with a family past that might otherwise remain unrecorded.

About the project

A family archive in conversation

This individual public-history project brings the experience of one family into dialogue with the social history of Greece in the late 1940s.

The story

My grandfather, father and uncle left Dipotamos, Kilkis, and worked in public labour in the Serres region during the Greek Civil War. The photographs also preserve moments of happiness, friendship and everyday life.

Where and when?

Dipotamos, Kilkis, and Vrontou in the Serres region, Greece, during the Greek Civil War (1946–1949), with family photographs identified as dating from 1949.

Why it matters

By connecting family memories with local history, the project records the experiences of people whose daily work, migration, family ties and moments of joy may be absent from more familiar accounts of the Civil War.

Method and format

A short subtitled video based on family photographs and a recorded interview with the author's aunt on 25 September 2026, presented here in an accessible text-and-image exhibition adaptation.
