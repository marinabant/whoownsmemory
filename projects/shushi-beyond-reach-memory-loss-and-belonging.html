Shushi / Shusha and memory

SHUSHI

BEYOND REACH

SEP 18, 2026

Memory, Loss and Belonging

Note on terminology. This project uses the Armenian name Shushi. In Azerbaijani, the city is

called Shusha. I use Shushi because this story focuses on Armenian collective memory, cultural

heritage, and my own relationship to the city; the terminology reflects that perspective.

Shushi is physically inaccessible to Armenians today. But this inaccessibility is not only about no

longer being able to go there. With the loss of the city, the Armenian historical and cultural

continuity formed in Shushi over centuries has been disrupted. Some of the material layers that

carried this continuity and memory have already been destroyed or altered, while others are at risk

of destruction.

03

The material environment of memory

This story is not an attempt to present the entire history of Shushi. It seeks to understand what

happens to collective memory formed around a place when physical connection with that place is

broken, while the material environment that carries that memory is destroyed or altered and its

historical identity is distorted.

Content note. This story contains references to mass violence, forced displacement, and

the destruction of cultural heritage.

WHEN NOT ONLY URBAN LIFE, BUT AN ENTIRE ENVIRONMENT IS DISRUPTED

The new rupture in Armenian presence and cultural continuity in Shushi as a result of the

2020 war had a historical precedent. A century earlier, the city had also experienced a

fundamental rupture in Armenian presence and cultural continuity.

In the nineteenth century, Shushi developed into an important Armenian educational,

intellectual, publishing, and cultural centre. Schools, printing houses, bookshops, and other

institutions operated in the city. A printing house was established there at the end of 1827,

and Armenian-language books were already being printed in 1828. In the following decades,

Shushi’s publishing and educational life became an important part of the city’s cultural

environment.

04

Schools, education and cultural life

One of the material witnesses to that world is the Shushi Real School, which opened in

1881 and operated until 1920. It was one of the key institutions in the city’s educational life.

Many of its students later continued their education at higher educational institutions in

Russia and Europe and became active in science, culture, professional fields, public life,

and politics.

The building of the Real School is therefore more than an old architectural structure. It is a

material trace of an entire intellectual and social world.

A memorial plaque on a house in Shushi commemorates Martiros Sarukhanyan (1873–

1896), who was born there and is identified on the plaque as one of the leaders of the 1896

Van self-defence.

05

The rupture of 1920

July 2016]

In March 1920, the massacre of Shushi’s Armenian population and the burning of the

Armenian part of the city destroyed not only residential neighbourhoods but also an

established urban and cultural environment. Historian Gayane Makhmourian’s study, based

on contemporary testimonies and extensive archival material, documents the massacre of

civilians and the complete destruction of the Armenian part of the city.

1920 is not simply a historical background here. It is a point of rupture in urban, cultural,

and social continuity.

1992: RETURN, VICTORY AND REBIRTH IN ARMENIAN PUBLIC MEMORY

This historical experience helps explain why, in 1992, Shushi acquired a meaning in

Armenian collective memory that went far beyond its military significance.

In Armenian public memory, the events of May 1992 are remembered as the liberation of

Shushi and as one of the key victories of the first war. In the following years, the meaning of

Changing cultural landscapes

that victory became associated with return, reconstruction, and cultural rebirth.

The Shushi Museum of History and Local Lore opened on 28 May 1992. In later years,

museums, cultural institutions, exhibitions, educational programmes, and public events in

the city became part of its revived cultural life. The Shushi City History Museum collected

and presented materials relating to the city’s architecture, public figures, everyday life, and

nineteenth-century cultural environment.

Thus, after 1992, rebirth was not only about constructing buildings or restoring monuments.

It was also an attempt to reconnect with an interrupted historical continuity.

Together, the two images connect the memory of victory with the cultural and public life

that continued after it.

Shushi thus became a symbol not only of victory, but also of return and rebirth after an

earlier historical rupture.

08

The events of 2020

2020: A NEW RUPTURE

During the 2020 war, Azerbaijani forces took control of Shushi, and following the 9

November ceasefire agreement, the city remained under Azerbaijani control.

In Armenian collective memory, the loss of Shushi carried significance beyond the

loss of a single city. It disrupted a narrative that had developed over nearly three decades,

in which Shushi had come to represent return, reconstruction, and renewed urban life.

The loss of physical access was later accompanied by threats to the material foundations of

memory.

In December 2021, the International Court of Justice ordered Azerbaijan to take all

necessary measures to prevent and punish acts of vandalism and desecration affecting

Armenian cultural heritage, including churches, places of worship, monuments, landmarks,

cemeteries, and cultural artefacts.

One of the most visible examples of this process is the Church of St John the Baptist in

Shushi, known as Kanach Zham. Eduard Minasyan photographed it in July 2016 while it

was standing and restored. Satellite monitoring by Caucasus Heritage Watch documented

that the church was completely destroyed between 28 December 2023 and 4 April 2024.

The same organisation has also documented the complete destruction of the old Armenian

cemetery of Ghazanchetsots in Shushi. Monument Watch likewise documents processes

involving the destruction and alteration of the city’s Armenian cultural heritage and the

reassignment of its historical identity.

The rupture in the connection with Shushi is deeper than simply “you can no longer go

there.” It is not only the territory that has become inaccessible. The material environment

through which history and memory were preserved and transmitted is also being distorted or

destroyed.

WHEN THE CITY REMAINS IN PHOTOGRAPHS

But physical inaccessibility does not mean the end of memory.

10

Photographs and distance

My photographs from Shushi are a simple example. They were taken at a time when I could

enter the city, walk through its streets, visit its museums, see its historic buildings, and

experience its cultural life.

At the time, they were simply photographs taken during a visit. Today, their status has

changed.

Mkhitaryan, 2016]

They have become a visual memory of an inaccessible environment. They preserve not

only what a particular building looked like, but also the reality that these buildings were part

of the everyday, cultural, and historical environment of a living city.

Following the forced displacement of 2023, the digital environment has also become an

important space for preserving the cultural memory of Artsakh. The “Artsakh Digital Cultural

Home”, presented in 2026, was created to collect, preserve, and make accessible elements

of Artsakh’s intangible cultural heritage, including language, oral traditions, stories, rituals,

music, crafts, and other cultural layers. The project is also connected to the reality that, after

BEYOND REACH

The story of Shushi shows that the loss of a place can simultaneously be a territorial,

material, and mnemonic rupture.

In 1920, an already established Armenian urban, cultural, and intellectual environment was

disrupted. After 1992, the return of Shushi became associated in Armenian collective

memory with victory and rebirth, as well as with the restoration of interrupted historical

continuity. In 2020, that trajectory was disrupted again, while the loss of physical connection

with the city was accompanied by threats to its Armenian material and cultural layers.

But Shushi does not disappear because it has become inaccessible.

It continues to exist in memory, photographs, stories, research, and digital archives.

This is where Beyond Reach takes on its central meaning: a place may be beyond

physical reach, but the question remains of how to preserve the historical, cultural,

and human connection with it when even the material foundations of that connection

are at risk.

Places, memory and interpretation

The story is situated in Shushi, in Nagorno-Karabakh/Artsakh, and moves across four interconnected temporal layers: 1920, 1992, 2020 and the present. It focuses on Armenian collective memory and cultural heritage: the rupture of the Armenian urban and cultural environment in 1920; the way the events of 1992 came to be remembered in Armenian public memory through ideas of return, victory and rebirth; the loss of Armenian physical access to the city after the 2020 war; and the post-2023 context of displacement and digital memory preservation.

Public-history question

How does collective memory persist when access to a place and its material traces are lost?
