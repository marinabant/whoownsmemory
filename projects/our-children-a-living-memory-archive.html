About the project

Our Children | আমাদের শিশু — A Living Memory Archive is a digital public-memory project examining what happens when a child-related tragedy becomes intensely visible in the social-media public sphere and then gradually recedes from collective attention. The submitted project presents one completed pilot episode based on the digital public that formed around the Apratim case in Bangladesh in September 2026. It draws on a digital-ethnographic archive containing 26 public source items and 12,150 Facebook comments and replies (11,091 main comments and 1,059 replies). Automated coding was used as a preliminary discovery layer; the public-facing project relies on selected material checked against source context and anonymised or paraphrased where necessary. The project traces mourning, blame and responsibility, justice claims, parenting and childhood discourse, social and moral diagnosis, and wider institutional interpretation. It deliberately avoids identifying images of minors, private family material, graphic imagery and identifiable ordinary social-media users. Its organising principle is: “The feed moves on. Memory should not.”

Historical setting

The story is situated in Bangladesh in September 2026 and is primarily digital. It follows the online public life of one child-related episode as it moved through Facebook, news reporting and wider public discussion. The project concerns the digital public that formed around the episode: people who mourned, interpreted, assigned responsibility, debated parenting and childhood, demanded justice and offered wider social or institutional explanations. The submitted public-history episode is deliberately ethically bounded. It does not reproduce identifying images of the child, private family photographs, graphic material or identifiable ordinary social-media users. Its focus is the production, circulation, fading and possible return of public memory.

Why this story matters

Digital platforms can make a tragedy intensely public while also making public attention unusually temporary. Posts, reactions, explanations and demands for justice accumulate rapidly, but another event can soon displace them. Visibility is therefore not the same as memory. If remembrance is left entirely to the rhythms of the feed, vulnerable experiences may disappear or survive mainly through decontextualised and repeatedly circulated imagery. Our Children experiments with another form of digital remembrance: preserving questions, public responses and evidentiary context while reducing renewed exposure and representational harm. It treats return—not endless recirculation—as part of responsible public memory.

The guiding question

What happens to public memory when the feed moves on? The project also asks: Who remains remembered after intense digital visibility disappears, which questions survive the attention cycle, and what responsibility does a public-memory archive have when returning to stories involving vulnerable people?

Project description

Our Children is a digital public-memory project examining what happens when a child-related tragedy becomes intensely visible in the social-media public sphere and then gradually recedes from collective attention. The submitted project presents one completed pilot episode within a longer-term living archive. Rather than reproducing victim imagery or creating a catalogue of cases, it follows how a digital public forms around an episode: people share, grieve, speculate, explain, assign responsibility, demand justice, and then move on as new events enter the feed.

The project draws on a digital-ethnographic archive assembled around the Apratim case in Bangladesh in September 2026. The research corpus contains 26 public source items and 12,150 Facebook comments and replies (11,091 main comments and 1,059 replies). Automated coding was used as a preliminary discovery layer; the public-facing project relies on selected material that is checked against source context and presented in anonymised or paraphrased form where necessary.

Where and when the story is situated

The story is situated in Bangladesh in September 2026 and is primarily digital. It follows the online public life of one child-related episode as it moved through social media, news reporting and public discussion. Its focus is not only the event itself, but the digital public that formed around it: mourning, blame, justice claims, parenting and childhood discourse, social and moral diagnosis, and wider political or institutional interpretation.

Digital-ethnographic method

Corpus 26 public source items; 12,150 comments and replies

Comment structure 11,091 main comments; 1,059 replies

Initial analysis Automated/preliminary first-pass coding used to identify possible patterns

Public-history evidence Selected items checked against source context; ordinary users anonymised; sensitive/searchable comments paraphrased

Epistemic rule Verified fact, reported allegation/public claim, observation and interpretation are kept separate

Ethical rule Public visibility is not treated as permission to reproduce harm

Why this story matters

Digital platforms can make tragedy intensely public while making public attention unusually temporary. Posts, reactions, explanations and justice claims accumulate rapidly; another event can then displace them. Visibility is therefore not the same as memory.

The project experiments with a different form of remembrance: preserving questions, public responses and evidentiary context while reducing renewed exposure and representational harm. It treats return - not endless recirculation - as part of responsible public memory.

Narrative logic of the pilot episode

Visibility → Public Reaction → Explanation → Justice Claims → Fading → Return

Publicly circulated missing-child notices and social-media posts used to reconstruct the initial phase of digital visibility. Identifying child imagery is not reproduced in the submitted pilot episode.

Source transparency

News reporting is used to establish and cross-check chronology. Social-media comments are treated as evidence of public discourse and participation, not as proof that the claims made by commenters are factually true. Preliminary automated category counts are not presented as definitive findings in the final public-history story.
