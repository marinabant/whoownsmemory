01

What remains after visibility?

Our Children (আমাদের শিশু) examines what happens when a tragedy involving a child becomes intensely visible in the social-media public sphere, then gradually recedes from collective attention.

Posts, reactions, explanations and demands for justice can accumulate rapidly. Yet another event may soon displace them. The project begins from a distinction central to contemporary public history: visibility is not the same as memory.

Rather than creating a catalogue of cases or reproducing images of victims, the archive follows the public life of an episode: how people share, grieve, speculate, explain, assign responsibility and demand justice, and how those responses change as the feed moves on.

“The feed moves on. Memory should not.”Project’s guiding principle

The submitted contribution is a completed pilot episode within a longer-term living archive. Its central question is simple but demanding: What happens to public memory when the feed moves on?

02

A digital public forms

The pilot focuses on public responses surrounding the Apratim case in Bangladesh in September 2026.

The historical setting is digital as well as geographical. The episode moved through Facebook, news reporting and public discussion, generating many forms of engagement: grief and mourning, blame and responsibility, demands for justice, interpretations of parenting and childhood, and wider moral, social and institutional questions.

The project studies the digital public that formed around the event, not just the reported event itself. Its subject is the production and circulation of public memory, the disappearance of attention, and the possibility of returning to earlier questions without renewing harm.

In the submitted research, public news articles were used to establish and cross-check chronology; social-media commentary was treated as evidence of public response, not proof that commenters’ factual allegations were true.

03

Following digital traces

The pilot draws on an author-assembled digital-ethnographic corpus: 26 public source items and 12,150 linked Facebook comments and replies.

Of these contributions, 11,091 were main comments and 1,059 were replies. Automated, preliminary coding served as a first pass to identify possible patterns. It was a discovery layer, not a final verdict on the meaning or prevalence of particular responses.

For the public-facing narrative, selected material was checked against the source context. Ordinary commenters were anonymised; sensitive or readily searchable quotations were paraphrased. Raw identifiers and sensitive material remain within the restricted research archive rather than being reproduced here.

04

From attention to return

The pilot is organised as a sequence, tracing how an episode enters public attention and what becomes of it afterwards.

Narrative logic of the submitted pilot
VisibilityNews and public notices make the event widely seen.
Public reactionPeople share, mourn, respond and participate.
ExplanationAccounts, assumptions and interpretations circulate.
Justice claimsCalls for responsibility and accountability emerge.
FadingOther events compete for attention; the feed moves forward.
ReturnThe archive revisits questions in context and with care.

The six stages describe the project’s narrative structure; they are not a measured timeline of how every individual user responded.

Its interpretive aim is to preserve the questions raised by public attention, not to repeat the same distressing materials indefinitely. The archive asks what a community remembers after the period of intense circulation, whose experiences fade and which unresolved public questions still matter.

05

Remembering without reproducing harm

The project makes ethical withholding part of its public-history method.

The submitted pilot does not reproduce identifying images of minors, private family photographs, graphic imagery, or identifiable ordinary social-media users. Usernames and personal identifiers are withheld; sensitive or searchable comments are anonymised or paraphrased. Allegations and speculation are not presented as established facts.

The author describes a correction principle: where a factual, privacy or rights concern arises, the affected material should be corrected or removed. Verified information, reported allegations, digital-public observation and interpretation are distinguished throughout the research approach.

Curatorial note · Why the absence of images matters

No images of the child, screenshots of private individuals, or reproductions of public comments are displayed on this page. This follows the submitted project's publication approach. The absence is an editorial decision about dignity, privacy and the risk of recirculating harm, not a gap to be filled with illustrative stock photography.

The larger Our Children archive is designed to remain open to future returns to earlier stories. The submission to Who Owns Memory? is, however, presented as one finished pilot episode. The public Facebook page is linked directly, rather than embedded as a live feed that could display new or unreviewed content.

“We remember without reproducing the violation.”Project’s ethical principle

Bengali remains central to the Bangladesh-facing archive. The core explanation is provided here in English for the international exhibition audience.

AI-use and editorial responsibility disclosure
During the project’s development, generative AI was used for editorial, organisational and translation support. According to the author’s submission, AI output was not treated as historical evidence and was not used to fabricate quotations, testimonies, sources or archival materials, or to determine the factual status of claims. Historical references and interpretations were reviewed by the author, who retains responsibility for verification, ethical judgement and publication. Sensitive raw data and private family materials were not intentionally provided to public AI services.
