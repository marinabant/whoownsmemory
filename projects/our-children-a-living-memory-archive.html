The story

Our Children | আমাদের শিশু — A Living Memory Archive is a digital public-memory project examining what happens when a child-related tragedy becomes intensely visible in the social-media public sphere and then gradually recedes from collective attention. The submitted project presents one completed pilot episode based on the digital public that formed around the Apratim case in Bangladesh in September 2026. It draws on a digital-ethnographic archive containing 26 public source items and 12,150 Facebook comments and replies (11,091 main comments and 1,059 replies). Automated coding was used as a preliminary discovery layer; the public-facing project relies on selected material checked against source context and anonymised or paraphrased where necessary. The project traces mourning, blame and responsibility, justice claims, parenting and childhood discourse, social and moral diagnosis, and wider institutional interpretation. It deliberately avoids identifying images of minors, private family material, graphic imagery and identifiable ordinary social-media users. Its organising principle is: “The feed moves on. Memory should not.”

Where and when?

The story is situated in Bangladesh in September 2026 and is primarily digital. It follows the online public life of one child-related episode as it moved through Facebook, news reporting and wider public discussion. The project concerns the digital public that formed around the episode: people who mourned, interpreted, assigned responsibility, debated parenting and childhood, demanded justice and offered wider social or institutional explanations. The submitted public-history episode is deliberately ethically bounded. It does not reproduce identifying images of the child, private family photographs, graphic material or identifiable ordinary social-media users. Its focus is the production, circulation, fading and possible return of public memory.

Central public-history question

What happens to public memory when the feed moves on? The project also asks: Who remains remembered after intense digital visibility disappears, which questions survive the attention cycle, and what responsibility does a public-memory archive have when returning to stories involving vulnerable people?

Why does this story matter?

Digital platforms can make a tragedy intensely public while also making public attention unusually temporary. Posts, reactions, explanations and demands for justice accumulate rapidly, but another event can soon displace them. Visibility is therefore not the same as memory. If remembrance is left entirely to the rhythms of the feed, vulnerable experiences may disappear or survive mainly through decontextualised and repeatedly circulated imagery. Our Children experiments with another form of digital remembrance: preserving questions, public responses and evidentiary context while reducing renewed exposure and representational harm. It treats return—not endless recirculation—as part of responsible public memory.

Sources & evidence

Contemporaneous public missing-child notices and public social-media posts/comments, used to reconstruct digital visibility and public response. Source URLs and contextual information are retained in the research evidence register. Social-media comments are treated as evidence of public discourse, not as evidence that commenters’ factual claims are true.
