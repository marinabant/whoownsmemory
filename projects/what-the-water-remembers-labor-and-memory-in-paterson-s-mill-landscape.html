What the Water Remembers: Labor and Memory in Paterson's Mill Landscape

The story

What the “Water Remembers” is a photo essay centered on a visit to the Great Falls of the Passaic River and the surviving mill buildings of Paterson, New Jersey, in the United States. Prompted by an encounter with a textile lantern slide at the University of Connecticut’s Archives & Special Collections, I visited Paterson’s Great Falls and surviving mill buildings, bringing photographs of this post-industrial landscape into conversation with an archival stereograph of silk workers to explore how mill labor and industrialization shaped workers’ lives, communities, and the river’s ecosystem. It connects this history to contemporary debates about AI’s energy and water demands, inviting reflection on how technological change depends on both human labor and natural resources.

Where and when?

The story is situated at the Great Falls of the Passaic River and the surviving mill buildings of Paterson, New Jersey, in the northeastern United States. It brings an archival stereograph from around 1914 into conversation with photographs from my September 2026 visit. Its historical context includes the 1913 silk workers’ strike, supported by the Industrial Workers of the World, whose demands included an eight-hour day. The project concerns textile workers, including women and immigrant communities, whose labor sustained industrial production but whose individual lives are often missing from public memory. Rooted in Paterson, it takes a transnational perspective: textile production, migration, and struggles over working conditions connected mill communities across national borders. Its ecological focus considers how industrialization transformed rivers and the communities dependent on them, connecting that history to contemporary questions about the energy and water demands of AI. This is a place-based digital photo essay with transnational significance, rather than a comparative history of mills worldwide.

Central public-history question

How can Paterson’s Great Falls and surviving mills make visible the workers and ecological costs often overlooked in industrial heritage—and connect this local landscape to worldwide questions about labor, rivers, and the demands of technological change today?

Why does this story matter?

Paterson’s Great Falls and surviving mills raise an urgent question for today: what do we overlook when we celebrate technological innovation without accounting for the workers and natural resources that sustain it? Industrial heritage often foregrounds buildings and inventions, leaving working conditions and ecological consequences less visible. Today, AI’s demands for electricity and cooling water renew questions that reach beyond Paterson: who benefits from technological change, who bears its costs, and how do we protect the communities and ecosystems on which production depends? This story matters because remembering industrial labor and respecting rivers are not only concerns about the past. They belong at the center of decisions about our technological future.
