Subverting the Tracks: Refugee Agency and the 1943 Feldkirch Freight Train Escape

A railway station on the Austrian–Swiss border becomes a point of departure for an account of refugee agency under Nazi persecution. Photographs of Feldkirch today invite visitors to consider what remains visible—and invisible—in the railway landscape.

Encounter the story

History through places, objects and voices

The forgotten history of the 1943 Feldkirch Freight Train Escape reveals the current German- centric gap in Holocaust education. Through this example, a narrative of active subversion in intense surveillance networks is discovered. By reflecting on the public invisibility of this physical resistance in a relatively unknown city in mainstream Holocaust education, this calls for the essential digital public intervention that shatters the passive-victim stereotype and allows for the Holocaust to be taught as a globalized event.

The chapters below bring the contributor’s sources and images into a readable, illustrated public-history narrative. Photographs and visual records can be enlarged by selecting them.

Central public-history question

Chapter 01

A railway landscape and the question of agency

The European rail network played a vital role in the “Final Solution,” set out against European

Jews by the Nazis. Using both freight and passenger cars, the Germans transported millions of

people to killing centers in German-occupied Poland and Soviet Union. Many oral histories from

Holocaust survivors describe the conditions on the trains as not being provided with food or

Following the border traces

refugees entering their country (St. Louis Kaplan Feldman Holocaust Museum). Fueled by

antisemitism, on October 5th, 1938, the Reich Ministry of the Interior invalidated all German

passports held by Jews. The German government began requiring Jewish people to identify

themselves in a way that would separate them from the rest of the population. All German Jews

were now legally required to carry identity cards, their passports were stamped with a red letter

“J,” and Jews with first names of “non-Jewish” origin had to add “Israel” and “Sara” (United

States Holocaust Memorial Museum “German Jews’ Passports Declared Invalid”). This act

further increased the intense surveillance network that Jews were actively resisting, causing

refugees to have to find other avenues of escape.

Chapter 04

Feldkirch: a local encounter

Photograph of Feldkirch’s End of Built-Up Area Sign, 1 June 2026. Author’s personal collection.

An example of this escape avenue due to the increased surveillance at the borders happened on

January 7th, 1943, in Feldkirch, a small town on the Swiss border. Fourteen Jewish refugees were

taken off a coal train after officials became suspicious when wagon SZ 17068 was overloaded,

and the sliding door was unable to be opened. The main customs assistant, Reithmeyer, noted at

the main customs office that the refugees “covered their faces with scarves to protect them from

the frost and cold” and were wearing many layers of clothes. He further noted that they resisted

but were eventually forced to crawl out from beneath the coal at gunpoint. After being

questioned in detail, Reithmeyer states that the refugees explained they “boarded the wagons in

Slovakia on 30 December 1942 in order to avoid being deported to a labor camp” (Loewy). This

incident, among others like an escape organization in Medzilaborce that aided in Jews escaping

to Switzerland, at the border crossing between Austria and Switzerland gives a primary example

of the Jewish resistance within the railway system. By focusing on a lesser-known town in

Austria, compared to major cities that are often the focus in Holocaust education, this allows for

an expanded understanding of the Holocaust and the other cities and countries involved.

Acts of resistance that occurred at small border towns are often completely unheard of in most

classrooms. By not including these histories, important examples of Jewish resourcefulness and

agency are erased. It is essential that Holocaust education shatters the passive-victim stereotype

by proving that the German railways were also a space of Jewish agency and attempted global

escape. There has been a call for teachers to educate students on Jewish resistance during the

Holocaust by using real memoirs and events that focus on various forms of resistance. One

teacher at a Jewish Day Schools stressed “the importance and prevalence of different forms of
