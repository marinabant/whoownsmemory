About the project

English

In 1954, more than 14,000 Chinese prisoners of war from the Korean War came to Taiwan and were publicly represented as “Anti-Communist Heroes” within Taiwan’s Cold War political narrative. Decades later, their experiences of war, displacement, political ideology and resettlement remain not only in official archives, but also in personal testimonies, gestures, silences and ageing bodies. Drawing on my socially engaged art practice with former POWs at the Taipei Veterans Home between 2014 and 2018, this story explores the body as a living archive through which established public narratives can be revisited and complicated. It asks who has the authority to decide how these men should be remembered: the state, the archive, or those whose bodies and memories carry this history themselves?

Chinese 1954年，一萬四千多名中國籍韓戰戰俘來到台灣，並在台灣的冷戰政治敘事中被塑造成「反共義士」。數十年後，他們對戰爭、流離失所、政治意識形態與重新安置的經驗，不只存在於官方檔案，也留存在個人的證言、動作、沉默與逐漸老去的身體之中。透過我2014年至2018年間與台北榮民之家前韓戰戰俘共同進行的社會參與式藝術實踐，這個故事將「身體」視為一座活的檔案，藉此重新觀看並複雜化既有的公共歷史敘事。它進一步追問：究竟誰有權決定這些人應該如何被記憶？是國家、檔案，還是那些以自己的身體與記憶承載這段歷史的人？

Historical setting

The story is situated between the Korean War and contemporary Taiwan, with particular attention to the arrival of Chinese Korean War POWs in Taiwan in 1954 and their later lives. It focuses on former POWs who settled in Taiwan, particularly those I encountered through socially engaged art projects at the Taipei Veterans Home between 2014 and 2018. The story connects Cold War history with their later-life memories, testimonies and embodied experiences.

Why this story matters

The “Anti-Communist Heroes” were once highly visible political symbols in Cold War Taiwan, yet the complexity of their individual experiences has become far less visible. Revisiting their lives through testimony, artistic practice and embodied memory invites audiences to consider how states, archives and individuals produce different versions of the past. Beyond Taiwan, the story contributes to wider conversations about Cold War history, displacement and the politics of memory in East Asia. It ultimately raises a question relevant to public history across different contexts: who has the authority to determine how difficult histories are remembered?

The guiding question

Who has the authority to decide how Korean War POWs in Taiwan should be remembered: the state, the archive, or the people whose bodies and memories carry this history?
