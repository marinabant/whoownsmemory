Memoria Viva : Aqui no se rinde nadie (nobody gives up here)

Elena Hernandez Camero•Digital exhibition / web story, Audio story / podcast, I ended up deciding that rather than an audio short, an exhibition format would suit the clips better.•Solo

The story

The project is a digital exhibition featuring four extracts from an interview with Rosa Garcia Alcon. Rosa is a memory and justice activist for the right to truth, justice and reparation for Francoist crimes. She is also actively engaged in memory work around the struggle for democracy and social justice during late-francoism, which she was part of as part of the student movement, for which she was tortured and imprisoned. The exhibition explores one perspective from someone working to make their experiences visible, to seek justice, and to seek how the present and future are understood through a recognition of the past. The focus is on Rosa’s reflections about doing that process and the choices and practices underlying it. What lies behind memory work as an ongoing collective and future-oriented practice, rather than a recollection of the past? What guides the reconstruction and transmission of difficult memories? Importantly, this exploration takes place against a context of ongoing institutionalised impunity, which has led to Spain being repeatedly admonished for its international obligations failings. As such, this is a story about memory as pushing against/through institutionalised silence/impunity and how that is navigated and negotiated.

Where and when?

Spain, memory and justice activism work. This work directly concerns the historical context of individuals who were victims in one way or another to Francoist crimes, in particular late Francoist crimes and due to their political dissidence. Although it relates to events from the dictatorship, this movement only started after decades of silence, in the 2010s. It is an ongoing process. Additionally, Rosa’s memory and justice work is not centred around solely her experience of the struggle for democracy and social justice, but rather expands through/across. For instance, La Comuna organises feminist series at a local venue where diverse women's histories during the dictatorship have been shared. Noteworthy, the content of the exhibition does not only concern a Spanish community insofar as it explores the process of memory activism, the methods and decisionmaking underpinning it. This is connected to, can be motivated by, and be facing obstacles shared with other struggles for justice and memory about difficult stories across the globe. Questions like “how can the transmission of memory interrupt the transmission of fear,” how is dignity influenced or not by ongoing impunity, and in which way does one mold memory are themes that cut across the Spanish experience, as well as others. Therefore, when conceiving the exhibition’s structure and content, it was not solely aimed at Spanish / Spanish interested audiences , but considers Rosa’s reflections as meaningful for a broader audience, whether that may be other activists conducting memory-building or other kinds of activist work, and/or societies shaped by difficult stories. This means that the website includes a level of explanation about the Spanish impunity model and memory activism. These are not widespread knowledge in Spanish society, which remains highly divided, and so I think the introductory information contextualising the audio clips throughout the exhibition is important for different kinds of audiences, for different reasons. This also responds and respects the original interview material, as the interview was conducted for an oral history collection specifically exploring forms of DIY activism. If anything else is needed for this question as I feel it’s quite broad so I could’ve approached it in different ways feel free to reach out and I’m happy to fill in anything concrete required!

Central public-history question

What lies behind memory work as an ongoing collective and future-oriented practice, rather than a recollection of the past? - Spanish struggle for memory and justice regarding the Francoist dictatorship through the perspective of one activist.

Why does this story matter?

This story matters because it explores memory as constructed by an agent who experienced that memory that is being built. It’s thus important insofar as it’s a direct testimony of how someone reflects about and reclaims their own memories of struggle for democracy and against impunity for crimes committed by a dictatorship. It is also important because for Rosa, as for other members of La Comuna, truth, justice and reconciliation are joined together and are collective and future-oriented in nature. This means they understand justice/truth as something that ought to contribute towards no repetition/transformation, as a continuation of struggle for social justice and as something that is passed down and continues throughout generations. These notes come from my own human righsts thesis, which asked justice activist for late francoist memory what is justice, what does it require and why. I think it’s important to gain insight into how does memorybuilding look like and is reflected by someone who is coming from that point of view as well as being first-hand experience agent, that is what they term “revolutionary memory/alive memory.” I think it also matters with regards to hearing about reflections about the process of memorybuilding activism itself. For instance, the experience of bringing lawsuits to Spanish domestic courts despite it being considered very unlikely to be successful in bringing down judicial impunity, which still continues, talks about the decisionmaking of not only pursuing one movement’s own definition of truth/justice regardless of how possible it may seem, and how it may differ from the majority of society or the state’s, but also about understanding memorybuilding as an open-ended path. One thing may lead to another, and another to yet another thing that you could not foresee. As with this, I think her testimonies throughout the extracts are, like she says when talking about the young people the speak to, “necessary and capable.” Broadly/more generally, fifty-one years of democracy in, I think the Spanish struggle explored through this story can highlight that justice and truth should be a precondition of democratic legitimacy, not its afterthought. If in addition to being used for the reviewing of the story by the WOM team, this or any of the above answers is to be displayed or shared for context of the project I’d like to have the opportunity to revise them with that in mind.
