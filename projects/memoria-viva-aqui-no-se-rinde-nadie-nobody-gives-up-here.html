Memoria Viva: Aquí no se rinde nadie (Nobody Gives Up Here)

A digital oral-history exhibition featuring interview extracts with Rosa García Alcón about activism, everyday repression under Francoism, democratic memory and the struggle for truth, justice and reparation.

About the project

A digital oral-history exhibition featuring interview extracts with Rosa García Alcón about activism, everyday repression under Francoism, democratic memory and the struggle for truth, justice and reparation.

Historical setting

Spain, memory and justice activism work. This work directly concerns the historical context of individuals who were victims in one way or another to Francoist crimes, in particular late Francoist crimes and due to their political dissidence. Although it relates to events from the dictatorship, this movement only started after decades of silence, in the 2010s. It is an ongoing process. Additionally, Rosa’s memory and justice work is not centred around solely her experience of the struggle for democracy and social justice, but rather expands through/across. For instance, La Comuna organises feminist series at a local venue where diverse women's histories during the dictatorship have been shared. Noteworthy, the content of the exhibition does not only concern a Spanish community insofar as it explores the process of memory activism, the methods and decisionmaking underpinning it. This is connected to, can be motivated by, and be facing obstacles shared with other struggles for justice and memory about difficult stories across the globe. Questions like “how can the transmission of memory interrupt the transmission of fear,” how is dignity influenced or not by ongoing impunity, and in which way does one mold memory are themes that cut across the Spanish experience, as well as others. Therefore, when conceiving the exhibition’s structure and content, it was not solely aimed at Spanish / Spanish interested audiences, but considers Rosa’s reflections as meaningful for a broader audience, whether that may be other activists conducting memory-building or other kinds of activist work, and/or societies shaped by difficult stories. This means that the website includes a level of explanation about the Spanish impunity model and memory activism. These are not widespread knowledge in Spanish society, which remains highly divided, and so I think the introductory information contextualising the audio clips throughout the exhibition is important for different kinds of audiences, for different reasons. This also responds and respects the original interview material, as the interview was conducted for an oral history collection specifically exploring forms of DIY activism. If anything else is needed for this question as I feel it’s quite broad so I could’ve approached it in different ways feel free to reach out and I’m happy to fill in anything concrete required!

Why this story matters

This story matters because it explores memory as constructed by an agent who experienced that memory that is being built. It’s thus important insofar as it’s a direct testimony of how someone reflects about and reclaims their own memories of struggle for democracy and against impunity for crimes committed by a dictatorship. It is also important because for Rosa, as for other members of La Comuna, truth, justice and reconciliation are joined together and are collective and future-oriented in nature. This means they understand justice/truth as something that ought to contribute towards no repetition/transformation, as a continuation of struggle for social justice and as something that is passed down and continues throughout generations. These notes come from my own human rights thesis, which asked justice activist for late francoist memory what is justice, what does it require and why. I think it’s important to gain insight into how does memory-building look like and is reflected by someone who is coming from that point of view as well as being first-hand experience agent, that is what they term “revolutionary memory/alive memory.” I think it also matters with regards to hearing about reflections about the process of memory-building activism itself. For instance, the experience of bringing lawsuits to Spanish domestic courts despite it being considered very unlikely to be successful in bringing down judicial impunity, which still continues, talks about the decisionmaking of not only pursuing one movement’s own definition of truth/justice regardless of how possible it may seem, and how it may differ from the majority of society or the state’s, but also about understanding memory-building as an open-ended path. One thing may lead to another, and another to yet another thing that you could not foresee. As with this, I think her testimonies throughout the extracts are, like she says when talking about the young people the speak to, “necessary and capable.” Broadly/more generally, fifty-one years of democracy in, I think the Spanish struggle explored through this story can highlight that justice and truth should be a precondition of democratic legitimacy, not its afterthought. If in addition to being used for the reviewing of the story by the WOM team, this or any of the above answers is to be displayed or shared for context of the project I’d like to have the opportunity to revise them with that in mind.

The guiding question

What lies behind memory work as an ongoing collective and future-oriented practice, rather than a recollection of the past? - Spanish struggle for memory and justice regarding the Francoist dictatorship through the perspective of one activist.

The project story

This participant did not supply usable archival images with the material available for this rebuild. No substitute historical photographs have been invented.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 02

And it’s the same with us.

“Why were we arrested?”

We were arrested because we wanted to put an end to a dictatorship.

“What was a dictatorship?”

Young people ask me this. At least, they always say to me, “But how did you fight?”

Because, of course, they have a concept of… “What is a dictatorship? What is it based on?”

So you have to explain to them that a dictatorship isn’t just that they would shoot you.

It’s that they wouldn’t even let you walk on the grass, which is something I always tell because it used to drive me crazy, why shouldn’t I be able to walk on the grass?

That kind of subtle repression, which seeps into, and seeped into, the whole of society.

And, of course, the whole issue of what we women, the girls, the young people, went through.

You reached adolescence and you had to learn ways of defending yourself against everything.

If you went on the Metro or on a bus, you had to know how to defend yourself.

We had techniques that we passed on to one another.

“Put your elbows together like this, then you do this…”

A friend’s mother used to carry a pin, and when someone came too close to her, she would give him a, a jab with the pin!

Those kinds of things that we have normalized and naturalized, and we don’t think of as being part of memory, but they are part of memory.

A documentary came out recently about the women who decided to play football, and the beating they were given.

Well, those are the kinds of things that… That brings it down to earth.

You’re not talking about the great heroes and heroines, with the flag, “oooh, oooh.”

No, you bring it down to earth, to earth. Which is where you need to be. For people to understand it.

So that is the memory we are building, the memory of the small things.

Because that’s how they take it in, they consider it their own.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 03

You can’t tell them, “Look, to fight against a dictatorship, you have to stand there and let yourself be arrested and tortured…”

No, because they did that to us against our will.

We did not choose that.

We chose the other part. This was a consequence, but we did not choose it.

That’s why I always say, “I went to the DGS because they forced me.”

I don’t know if I told you this.

When the military judge came to the DGS (General Directorate of Security) to take my statement, and then he left, the captain stayed behind, who was the one acting as the secretary.

He must have been doing his military service.

He had this terrified look on his face…

Then the captain said to me, “We’re here because we’re being forced to.”

And I said, “Me too! Why do you think I’m here?”

[Laughs]

It’s a bit like that.

Not to downplay the issue of repression, no. But yes to put it in the context that this is the way those in power try to stop struggles: repression.

But it feeds back into itself.

When there is a lot of struggle, there is a lot of repression (...)

Giving them examples of what was done at that time, how we made the posters, how we put them up, how we transported the propaganda, where we hid it,

and how we fought back.

And how we faced the imposed prohibitions. Because throughout the whole of human history, youth have always confronted prohibitions, because that’s normal.

.How we didn’t respect the rules that were imposed on us and looked for loopholes, as young people, because that’s what young people normally do.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 04

DIGNITY

We hold/uphold on to that dignity, no matter what

What does that mean, to hold on to your dignity no matter what?

It means that we have/carry the pride of all the people who came before us

They had to have courage to go with a shotgun and confront such an army, they had to have courage to have fought in the villages, and to have “stood out” and to have defended what they believed in and paid for it. Paid dearly.

They must have had to have courage. And they did (...) and they were murdered and they had the dignity to die, many of them with their fists held high.

3.07.20 And we’re the generations that inherited’/received all of that.

And that we also decided that this dictatorship had to end already

That we did not want to go on living like that.

So we are the recipients of their dignity

And also, continue that dignity forward in our struggle, both during the dictatorship and afterwards during the transition.

So yes, our dignity is safe

and we want to pass it on to the next generation—

(...) Everywhere you look there are analyses -what’s going to happen, what’s not going to happen, this, that…

But what we are missing is someone who says: well, this is what we have to do.

Yes, fine. We are already clear that there are super-rich people, super-rich, who this time are accumulating more money and more of everything,

But “and”/so what? Then what?

We are missing this “and.”

That “and” is what we think matters, and we put in it dignity as important

The dignity of everything we’ve been recipients of/inherited, the dignity of everything we have to pass on to the next generations that come after us. You must not give up. Because you must not give up.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 05

3.08.50 I wish you could give up, but we simply cannot *(why do you say that you cannot give up)...(you cannot give up because after spending your whole life always on the side of the struggle, knowing that you have to fight to achieve the bare minimum, how are you going to give up now? It makes no sense. So, what you have ahead of you, you just keep going (...)

3.09.26 If we do not achieve it, others will. (...) Because the people they murdered after the war died knowing that there’d be others who would carry their ideas forward.

Most of them wrote it in their letters

The one from the process of 27 September 1975

(letter says) ‘They’re going to kill us, but there will be others who will fight’

And that… I, no I…

I got out of,

I survived,

I’ve got that task that they set (...)

They entrusted it to us:

‘They’re going to kill me, but there’ll be others who’ll keep fighting’

So that’s it.

This is a task that we have carried from generation to generation throughout all of history (...) you know that there are better periods and worse periods, that history is not linear, but in the worst periods is when you have to maintain hope the most, and this is the one that has fallen to us.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 06

FEAR

It is an ancestral fear, it is that trauma that has been passed down from generation to generation, which is very powerful.

Which surprises me because I come from a family that did not give fear wings, despite the fact that they went through it.

My grandfather is one of the disappeared, my uncle (...) I come from a family that has lost, but they did not pass that fear on to us,

and I have always downplayed it, but I have learned (through memory work/activism) that it’s like a weight pressing down on us. And it’s tied to impunity.

Because really if nobody is put on trial, all those people - those shameless ones, murderers, rapists (...) it was brutal.

All of that, if nobody has paid for any of it, if nobody has ever been put on trial

So how do you confront them? They are very powerful, they tell you.

Indeed, I have learned that. I’ve learned that the only way we can overcome that fear is if we manage -somehow- to put even one of the perpetrators on trial and bring them to justice,

which is why we have to insist.

That ongoing fear you’re talking about passed from generation to generation - how do you keep it in mind when talking to younger generations? How do you interrupt that cycle of fear when you give talks in high schools?

Because I put much more emphasis on what we did, not so much on what we suffered.

Okay, they detained us, okay, yes.

But what did we do, and how did we overcome that fear?

Because we were frightened. Very, very, very frightened.

But we had to overcome it.

So that is always what I transmit to them.

It is normal to be afraid, I mean, it is a defence mechanism, a basic instinct,

but we have to take into account that it can be overcome, controlled, or directed in a way that does not prevent you from moving, from responding.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 07

So that is always what I transmit to them:

if I, who was just a little 16- or 17-year-old girl, had the capacity to do these things that seem so extraordinary to them, even though they weren’t…

If I could do it, I had the capacity/was capable, why wouldn't they have it?

Because they are just the same (as us).

I said to one of the girls from the school the other day, you are valuable, you are necessary,

that is why we come to tell you [these things], because you are valuable and necessary. And you are capable.

Because it is true, if they do not do it, who is going to do it?

We are in a phase of decreasing, which is why we address young people.

Not only to share our testimony, but to pass on why our struggle is necessary, why it matters.

So that they adapt it to whatever they consider relevant, make it their own, in whatever way they think is right.

(...) The first thing is to understand what fear is;

You have to understand fear and not demonize it.

You’re not a coward because you’re afraid, you’re a rational human being.

The important thing is that you have to muster up strength

-which you have, it’s there- to combat that fear.

And having the trust and trusting that you, in effect, have that strength

And knowing that the fellow activist beside you will be there for you.

Power always does the same thing, well, so do we — which is to fight.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 08

THE MANY PATHS TO MEMORY

But because the Argentine lawsuit is being boycotted at the state level, the State Prosecutor General’s Office gave the order not to cooperate with Judge Servini, and the case was effectively stalled.

So we decided that we should bring the cases here (to Spain).

Because why not bring them within the Spanish state? Some people thought that if we filed lawsuits here in Spain, the Argentine case would be stopped. That hasn’t happened.

And there were also people saying, why even bother doing it here when we already know what the judiciary is like here?

But that didn’t go like that, because one of the things we did achieve—and it was a success—was that everyone came to know that there were torturers and that they were still alive, like González Pacheco, Billy the Kid, and El Muñecas, Captain Muñecas, because he was a captain in the Guardia Civil.

So that alone was already a tremendous success. When you ask people, Billy el Nino comes out.

Even in the Congress of Deputies—and this was Pablo Iglesias—he said, “the torturer Billy the Kid.” The torturer.

He was already being referred to as a torturer without ever having been tried, but only because of our testimonies.

That had never happened before (their activism).

This hadn’t happened before even when we put the lawsuit for the Argentinian Lawsuit

—well, it’s true that we had to bring the lawsuit here again; it wasn’t easy for any of us, we had to remember, and to go through that process, of remembering the events, again—but it was a success.

Not a single lawsuit against Billy the Kid was admitted, not one. But now everyone knew who he was, everyone. And given our possibilities, the truth is I consider it a tremendous success.

We continued with other torturers in Asturias and elsewhere. It served to make people aware that people were still being tortured at the end of Francoism and even after Franco’s death.

Those were the people who were fighting against the dictatorship. They were the ones who achieved democracy, not the King. That was a very important step.

FROM THE ORIGINAL PROJECT · 09

It’s true that you sometimes say, “What’s the point of going through everything you have to go through, only for them to tell you that they won’t admit the lawsuit?”

But that’s what political struggle is like. If we had it easy, that’s how it is, otherwise we wouldn’t be where we are
