The Many Portraits of Juan José Cabezudo

From Watercolour to Chatbot

Juan José Cabezudo (c

Laura Guien, Roland Alvarez Chavez, Elena Hernandez CameroSocial-media storytelling project

The public-history question
What survives of marginalized memories like Juan José Cabezudo's when young people ask an AI directly, without the sources and institutions that once contextualized his image? And what should they know before they trust the answer?
Why this story matters

For many young people, a chatbot is now the first point of contact with the past. Lives like Cabezudo's (Black, queer and working-class) entered the record only through other people's eyes, and each of those portraits carried its maker's bias. Historical images can still be questioned: they have an author, a date, a caption. A chatbot's answer has none, and it can change from one question to the next. By moving from historical visual representations to contemporary algorithmic representation, the project raises a warning without demonizing the technology: chatbots, like the painters before them, carry Cabezudo's memory forward, but never neutrally. The goal is to give young audiences, especially young LGBTQ+ people, the tools to ask who made the portrait they are looking at, including when it comes from a chatbot.

CHAPTER 01

Portraits are interpretations

Juan José Cabezudo, an Afro-Peruvian cook associated with early republican Lima, appears in a series of historical and contemporary visual representations. The project examines who made those portraits, who viewed them, and how each representation shaped the public memory of a person.

CHAPTER 02

From watercolour to photography

The visual record includes the watercolours of Francisco Javier Cortés and Pancho Fierro and later photographic and archival representations. These images are historical sources: they can preserve traces of a life, but they also reflect the interests, conventions and assumptions of their makers.

CHAPTER 03

From archival image to chatbot

A contemporary social-media carousel brings historical portraits into dialogue with queer memory work and the ways chatbots answer questions about the past. Its central problem is not simply which portrait is most recognisable, but who is authorised to construct a portrait in the first place.
