Exhibition introduction

How does a person become visible to history when almost every surviving account was produced by somebody else?

Juan José Cabezudo (c. 1800–1860) was an Afro-Peruvian cook in early republican Lima and is widely recognised in recent scholarship as an early publicly visible figure whose gender expression and sexuality challenged contemporary norms. His fame reached beyond his work with food: painters, writers and photographers recorded him, often through the conventions of satire and the classification of urban “types.”

This exhibition follows those changing images from nineteenth-century watercolours to studio photography, contemporary LGBTQ+ memory work and the answers produced by AI chatbots. It asks readers to look not only at what a portrait shows, but at who made it, why, and whose perspective remains absent.

Reading historical identity

“Queer” is a contemporary interpretive term, not a recorded statement of Cabezudo’s self-identification. The archival sources are uneven, and their language reflects the inequalities of their period. The exhibition does not treat those descriptions as neutral testimony.

01 / The historical record

A public figure, seen through other people’s eyes

Cabezudo left an unusually extensive visual trace for a person facing overlapping racial, class and gender hierarchies. Yet the abundance of portraits does not mean that the historical person is transparent to us. Watercolours, texts and photographs preserved a recognizable public figure while filtering that figure through their authors’ expectations.

As the creators observe, images have makers, dates and captions. Those details let us ask how a person was represented—and why the representation might have been flattering, comic, exoticizing or accusatory.

02 / The Cortés portraits · c. 1827

Clothing, gesture and the language of classification

Francisco Javier Cortés represented Cabezudo both in a street scene with a companion and as a standing figure in a separately captioned watercolour. Clothing, posture and urban setting become evidence of the social visibility that fascinated the painter. The inscriptions also demonstrate how representation could turn public recognition into a label imposed from outside.

The existence of both images complicates the idea of a single “authentic” portrait. Each selects different features. Both are mediated by the artist’s standpoint and by the social vocabulary of the time.

03 / The Fierro watercolours

Visibility does not mean neutrality

Pancho Fierro’s images helped carry Cabezudo’s reputation into the visual memory of Lima. In the creators’ reading, Fierro rendered Cabezudo with paler skin, illustrating how racial representation could be altered by the painter’s choices.

The paintings preserve a public figure and at the same time reproduce an insulting vocabulary. Their captions remain part of the historical source record, but they are not adopted as the exhibition’s own language.

04 / The studio photograph · c. 1860

A staged “type” for a changing city

Photography did not simply replace an artistic interpretation with a neutral likeness. The Courret studio arranged Cabezudo within the visual economy of nineteenth-century Lima, where photographs could be collected and circulated as images of recognizable local types.

The contributors describe this transformation into a picturesque tipo peruano. The studio setting, props, posture and caption all participate in the construction of the portrait. A photographic plate records a particular moment before a camera; it does not, by itself, explain the person pictured.

05 / Contemporary reclamation

A new portrait, made from a different position

Contemporary queer historical work does not merely rediscover Cabezudo. It changes the questions asked of the surviving record. The contributors place this project alongside Estampas maricas de Lima, a 2023 work by Roland Álvarez, Vero Ferrari and Carlos Jaramillo, illustrated by Mario Ríos. This work seeks to recover and reconstruct Lima’s histories of sexual dissidence from perspectives not centred on the old categories of ridicule.

The authors’ FINAL SUBMISSION records permission from the publisher and illustrator for a Cabezudo illustration used in the original carousel. As that separate artwork was not available as a verified source image for this webpage, it is credited here without an unauthorized substitute or a cropped screenshot.

Placing historic caricature beside present-day memory work shows why historical evidence and historical interpretation are not the same thing: the second must still reckon with the violence, omission and unequal power within the first.

Who gets to make a portrait when the historical person can no longer answer back?

06 / A contemporary experiment

What does a chatbot remember?

The team’s experiment asked whether the biases of historical representation persist in a new medium. According to the supplied project caption, on 21 September 2026 the researchers posed six neutral questions about Cabezudo’s life and legacy to six AI models, repeating each question three times with web search enabled.

Curatorial note

Reading images, naming harm

Some original nineteenth-century captions use maricón, a derogatory word tied to gender and sexual stigma. It appears here only where necessary to identify the historical artwork and examine its discriminatory framing. Its presence is not an endorsement. The exhibition retains these titles as archival evidence instead of quietly replacing them.

Comparing a watercolour, a photograph, a contemporary illustration and an AI-generated biography is a comparison of forms of representation, not a claim that they have equivalent evidentiary value. The images are traces produced in particular circumstances. The chatbot outputs are present-day responses about those traces. Neither should be confused with Cabezudo’s own voice.

Attributing historical queer identity requires care: different scholars use different terms, and there is no surviving self-description that settles the issue. Readers should consult the original sources and their contexts.

From the contributors

The original social-media caption

The complete accompanying caption supplied for this adaptation is retained below, with light corrections to English punctuation and spelling. It is presented as the authors’ interpretation; the research claims remain attributed to them.

Read the full caption supplied by the team

Juan José Cabezudo (c. 1800–1860) never had a neutral portrait.

An Afro-Peruvian cook in early republican Lima, Cabezudo is widely considered Peru’s first openly queer public figure: ‘hyper-visible but marginalized, mocked yet socially embedded’ (Making Queer History).

For a man at the margins of race, class and sexuality, he left an unusually rich trace in the arts of his time. But each of these traces carries the bias of its maker. Fierro paints him with paler skin. Courret stages him as a picturesque ‘tipo peruano’ for collectors.

Today, what happens when you ask a chatbot about Cabezudo?

On 21 September 2026, we put six neutral questions about Cabezudo’s life and legacy to six AI models (Claude Sonnet 5, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.5 Flash, GLM-5.3, Mistral Large, Grok 4.6), three times each, with web search on.

None of our questions mentioned his queerness.

Almost every complete answer brought it up (88 of 89), but never in the same way: sometimes in the first line, sometimes at the end, once not at all. Interestingly, the same model, asked the same question twice, gave a full account of his queerness, then a fluent biography with no trace of it. Each model also chose its own words:

‘openly homosexual’ (Claude); ‘gender-nonconforming’ (GPT-5.5); ‘queer-coded’ (Mistral).

Like the paintings and photographs before them, chatbots alter Cabezudo’s image. But those left an author, a date, a caption. A chatbot leaves none, and returns many versions, not all of them accurate. A memory that shifts, assembled at the moment you ask from a handful of web pages and the biases they carry.

This weighs heaviest on fragile memories like his (Black, queer, working-class) that barely entered the record, or only through other people’s eyes.

About the AI study: The chatbot answers shown in the original project are not sources about Juan José Cabezudo. We show them as objects of study: they reveal how AI systems reproduce, omit or reframe what is known about him.
