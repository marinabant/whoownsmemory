01 / The exhibition
How has the memory of the Warsaw Ghetto been constructed over time—and by whom?Lynne Feldman · Public-history question

A walk through the making of memory

Lynne Feldman’s Walking Memory is a place-based StoryMap situated in Warsaw, where the history of the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising meets the ways that history has been remembered. The project is conceived as a walk along the Hero’s Path: an invitation to encounter places of commemoration and ask how the past becomes visible in the city.

Its central concern is not only what the monuments commemorate, but how public memory is formed through decisions about design, location, narrative and emphasis. A memorial can make a history present; it can also direct attention toward certain experiences while leaving other questions unanswered.

By moving through the interactive map, visitors are invited to consider the different people, institutions and historical circumstances that contribute to a commemorative landscape.

02 / Historical setting

Warsaw: from persecution to remembrance

German occupation authorities established the Warsaw ghetto in 1940. A large Jewish community was forced into a confined, sealed-off district where hunger, disease and persecution caused immense suffering. Mass deportations to the Treblinka killing centre began in 1942.

On 19 April 1943, when German forces moved to liquidate the ghetto, Jewish fighters and civilians resisted. The uprising continued until 16 May 1943, when German forces crushed it and devastated the area. Historical overview: USHMM.

Feldman’s StoryMap begins from a different, later question: how this history is encountered through the streets, monuments and interpretive choices of Warsaw today.

04 / Questions for the visitor

Reading a landscape of remembrance

The original project asks visitors to look beyond what is commemorated and examine the decisions behind public memory.

01 / PLACE

Why here?

Consider how a monument’s site changes the way we encounter the events it represents. What can be learned from its surroundings?

02 / FORM

Who chose this representation?

Notice the words, figures, architectural forms and gestures used to communicate a historical event. Whose perspective might they prioritise?

03 / TIME

When did remembrance take shape?

Distinguish the dates of wartime events from the later moments when monuments and public narratives were created.

04 / ABSENCE

What remains difficult to see?

Ask which people, experiences and questions are illuminated by a memorial, and which may remain outside its frame.

Curatorial note

This page introduces the author’s original interactive StoryMap; it does not replace or transcribe the map’s individual slides. The interpretive prompts above are editorial reading aids based on the research question supplied by Lynne Feldman, not quotations from particular map stops.

Public commemoration is a form of historical interpretation. It can be studied through monument design, changes in the city, the timing of commemorative initiatives and the perspectives of the institutions and communities involved.
