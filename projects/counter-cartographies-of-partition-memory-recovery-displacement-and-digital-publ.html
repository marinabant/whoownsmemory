The story

Drawing on archives, feminist historiography, survivor memoirs, literary representations, and digital humanities, the project interrogates how the 1947 Partition has been remembered, regulated, and spatially narrated by the state and by those whose lives were displaced. It foregrounds the gendered violence and coercive logic of the Central Recovery Operation, places official records in dialogue with accounts such as Kamla Patel’s Torn from the Roots, and expands the geography of Partition memory through the Sindhi refugee experience in Indore and Madhya Bharat. The project ultimately examines how geospatial mapping, oral-history archives, virtual reality, and digital storytelling can recover marginalized and contradictory memories while preserving the ethical discomfort, silences, and complexities that official histories often erase.

Where and when?

The project is situated primarily in India and Pakistan in the aftermath of the 1947 Partition, with particular attention to the Central Recovery Operation of 1947–49 and its consequences for displaced and abducted populations. Its geographical focus moves between the Punjab–Delhi recovery corridor and refugee settlements in central India, especially Indore, Bhopal, Bairagarh, and the wider Madhya Bharat region, thereby extending beyond the conventional Punjab- and Bengal-centred histories of Partition. It concerns abducted women and children, Partition survivors, refugee and displaced communities, particularly the Sindhi diaspora, and the social workers and institutions involved in recovery and rehabilitation, while also engaging literary and testimonial representations of these experiences.

Central public-history question

Who owns the memory of Partition, and how can public history recover experiences that official archives have silenced without reproducing the same forms of coercion, simplification, and erasure? The project asks how state records, survivor testimony, literature, and spatial memory can be brought into dialogue to reveal the gap between official narratives of recovery and the lived, often contradictory experiences of those who were displaced or forcibly recovered.

Why does this story matter?

This story matters because Partition memory is still shaped disproportionately by national and official frameworks that can obscure the agency, trauma, and unresolved experiences of those most affected by displacement. By foregrounding abducted women, displaced communities, and the underexamined refugee geographies of central India, the project reveals that even purported acts of humanitarian recovery could reproduce displacement. Its digital-history dimension is equally important because emerging technologies can either flatten difficult histories into consumable narratives or create more critical, participatory spaces in which ambiguity, silence, and competing memories remain visible.
