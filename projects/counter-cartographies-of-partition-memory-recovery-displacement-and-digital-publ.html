Content note

This exhibition discusses abduction, displacement and gendered violence connected to the 1947 Partition.

01 / The argument

Whose memories appear on the map?

Who owns the memory of Partition, and how can public history recover experiences that official archives have silenced without repeating the same patterns of coercion and erasure?

Rudransh Kantharia’s project examines the 1947 Partition of British India and its aftermath through government policies, women’s testimonies, survivor memoirs, literature and the spatial histories of displacement.

A central concern is the gap between administrative accounts of “recovery” and the often contradictory experiences of people whose lives were regulated by those procedures. The project also considers how digital history can represent competing memories without flattening their differences.

02 / A state project

The Central Recovery Operation, 1947–1949

In the aftermath of Partition, India and Pakistan adopted arrangements for the recovery and restoration of abducted persons. The contributor examines the Inter-Dominion Conference at Lahore on 6 December 1947, the Indian Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act of 1949, and the legal record of The State of Punjab v. Ajaib Singh and Another.

These documents show how the state defined and administered recovery. Read with feminist historiography, they also invite a critical question: what happened when an official process of restoration conflicted with personal choices, relationships, or understandings of belonging?

Reading the archive

The words “recovery” and “restoration” belong to the historical legal and administrative vocabulary. The exhibition examines the language critically rather than treating it as a neutral description of people’s experiences.

03 / Lived experience

Women’s memories beyond official categories

Accounts by Kamla Patel, Anis Kidwai and later feminist historians place the consequences of Partition in a different register from legal paperwork. They reveal the tensions between family, kinship, gendered violence, humanitarian intervention and the right to tell one’s own story.

Kantharia’s argument holds these different types of record in conversation, showing that policies described as humanitarian could also produce experiences of coercion or renewed displacement.

Content note

This chapter concerns abduction, sexual and gendered violence during and after Partition. It does not reproduce graphic testimony or identify private survivors.

04 / Beyond the border corridor

Sindhi displacement and central India

The study widens its geographical attention beyond Punjab and Bengal to the movements and settlements of Sindhi refugees in central India, particularly Indore, Bhopal, Bairagarh and the wider Madhya Bharat region.

05 / Digital public history

Mapping against the grain

Geospatial storytelling, oral-history archives, virtual environments and digital interpretation can make suppressed or scattered sources easier to encounter. They can also reproduce familiar simplifications when a map suggests fixed borders, complete journeys or unambiguous identities where the evidence is unsettled.

The project argues for digital memory work that makes its own choices visible and acknowledges silence, uncertainty and multiple perspectives.
