01 / THE PUBLIC-HISTORY QUESTION

Who gets to draw the map of memory?

The 1947 Partition of British India is remembered through borders, states and displacement. Rudransh Kantharia’s project asks what comes into view when the map begins with people whose experiences do not fit easily into official accounts.

Drawing on state records, feminist histories, survivor memoirs, literature and digital humanities, Counter-Cartographies of Partition Memory examines how Partition has been remembered and regulated. Its focus includes the recovery of abducted women, the difficult distinction between restoration and coercion, and refugee histories beyond the best-known Partition geographies.

Rather than treating archival records or digital maps as neutral containers of truth, the project asks who produced them, whose perspectives they privilege, and what remains outside their frame.

Who owns the memory of Partition — and how can public history make neglected experiences visible without repeating the exclusions of the past?

02 / RECOVERY & AGENCY

When recovery could mean another displacement

Following Partition, India and Pakistan developed policies for locating and returning abducted people. The language of “recovery” suggested restoration, yet survivors’ circumstances and wishes were not always represented by official categories.

Kantharia places the Central Recovery Operation and the Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act, 1949 alongside women’s memories and feminist scholarship. This comparison makes visible tensions between state policy, community expectations, family ties and individual agency.

Memoirs such as Kamla Patel’s Torn from the Roots and histories by Urvashi Butalia, Ritu Menon and Kamla Bhasin offer perspectives that challenge a purely administrative account of rehabilitation.

03 / GEOGRAPHIES OF DISPLACEMENT

Looking beyond familiar borders

The project moves beyond the best-known Punjab- and Bengal-centred histories by considering refugee experiences in central India, especially the Sindhi diaspora.

Indore, Bhopal, Bairagarh and the wider Madhya Bharat region appear as points through which to ask how displacement reshaped places and belonging. Their inclusion widens the question of where Partition memory resides and who has access to narrating it.

04 / READING SOURCES CRITICALLY

Not all archives tell the same story

State correspondence and legislation document how officials defined and administered recovery. Memoirs and oral testimony can show how policies were experienced, contested or remembered differently. Kantharia reads these forms of evidence together without assuming that any one archive provides a complete account.

Four kinds of evidence in dialogue

Official records

Policy and law

Inter-Dominion discussions, the 1949 Act and legal proceedings frame the institutions and classifications used in recovery.

First-person accounts

Memoirs and testimony

Accounts including Kamla Patel’s Torn from the Roots complicate the meanings of return and restoration.

Critical scholarship

Gender and power

Feminist histories ask whose wishes and experiences were represented, sidelined or denied.

Digital public history

New routes to memory

Oral-history archives, digital storytelling and VR projects offer different ways to connect witnesses, place and public audiences.

05 / DIGITAL MEMORY

Digital tools do not resolve the ethical questions

Interactive mapping, oral-history archives and immersive projects can bring overlooked histories into view. They also raise questions about selection, representation, consent and the transformation of lived experience into a public narrative.

The project considers digital initiatives such as The 1947 Partition Archive and Project Dastaan alongside the opportunities and limitations of geospatial and immersive historical storytelling.

In this framing, a counter-cartography does more than offer another set of locations. It questions the assumptions that shape a map: what counts as evidence, which stories occupy the centre, and whether spaces are left for contradiction, uncertainty and silence.

A more inclusive digital map should not turn complicated histories into a single, tidy story.
