Counter-Cartographies of Partition Memory

Recovery, Displacement and Digital Public History

Drawing on archives, feminist historiography, survivor memoirs, literary representations, and digital humanities, the project interrogates how the 1947 Partition has been remembered, regulated, and spatially narrated by the state and by those whose lives were displaced

The public-history question
Who owns the memory of Partition, and how can public history recover experiences that official archives have silenced without reproducing the same forms of coercion, simplification, and erasure?
Why this story matters

This story matters because Partition memory is still shaped disproportionately by national and official frameworks that can obscure the agency, trauma, and unresolved experiences of those most affected by displacement. By foregrounding abducted women, displaced communities, and the underexamined refugee geographies of central India, the project reveals that even purported acts of humanitarian recovery could reproduce displacement. Its digital-history dimension is equally important because emerging technologies can either flatten difficult histories into consumable narratives or create more critical, participatory spaces in which ambiguity, silence, and competing memories remain visible.

CHAPTER 01

Mapping policies of recovery

Rudransh Kantharia examines the aftermath of the 1947 Partition of India, focusing on women’s experiences of abduction, recovery, displacement and state intervention. The project uses digital visualisation to question how official categories define what counts as return or restoration.

CHAPTER 02

Women’s lives beyond administrative categories

Research on the Abducted Persons (Recovery and Restoration) Act, 1949, alongside feminist and oral-history scholarship, shows how official recovery programmes intersected with personal autonomy, family ties and the aftermath of violence.

A counter-cartography of memory

By bringing scholarship into a public-facing visual presentation, the work challenges maps that treat borders and population movements as sufficient explanations for people’s experiences.
