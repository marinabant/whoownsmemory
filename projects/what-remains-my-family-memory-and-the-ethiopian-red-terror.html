Testimony & Family Memory

What Remains: My Family Memory and the Ethiopian Red Terror

Eden Matiyas Mulugeta•Object story / object essay•Solo

The story

What Remains: My Family Memory and the Ethiopian Red Terror explores how family memory survives when a person’s story is left largely untold. Through objects and fragments connected to my father’s imprisonment during Ethiopia’s Derg regime, including handmade socks, a handwritten poem, the number of his prison room, a lost embroidery, and a book he gave me, the project traces what remains of an experience I never fully knew. It reflects on silence, loss, memory, and the ways personal histories of political violence are carried across generations.

Where and when?

The story is situated in Ethiopia during the Derg regime and the Red Terror, particularly between 1975 and 1977, when my father was imprisoned in Kerchele Prison (Alem Bekagn) in Addis Ababa. It concerns his experience of imprisonment and the fragments of his story preserved through family memories and objects. The project connects this historical experience to intergenerational memory in the present.

Central public-history question

How do everyday objects preserve and transmit memories of political violence when those experiences are only partially told or recorded?

Why does this story matter?

The story makes a difficult national history accessible through personal materials that survived outside official archives. By focusing on one individual’s experience, it offers younger generations a more personal and tangible way to engage with a history they did not experience themselves. Individual stories can make the human impact of political violence more immediate while showing how private histories contribute to collective memory. The story also highlights the gaps and uncertainties of intergenerational memory, inviting audiences to consider what is remembered, what is lost, and what is passed on.

Sources & evidence

1. Bridget Conley’s Memory from the Margins: The Red Terror Martyrs’ Memorial Museum in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. 2. Amnesty International Report on Human Rights Violations in Ethiopia. 3.Remember Alem Bekagn Alex de Waal in Addis Ababa.

Public-history lens

This project is presented within Testimony, Family Memory & Community Archives. Read it not only for what happened in the past, but for how sources, memory, place, visibility and interpretation shape what becomes public history.
