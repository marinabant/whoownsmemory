01 / A transnational history

Two histories in dialogue

How did the Cold War shape migration experiences in Greece and Poland, and what connections can be drawn between them?

Inspired by their own family histories, Vicky Antoniou and Kacper Krzysztof Dziekan bring together two migration experiences: Greek political refugees who settled in Poland after the Greek Civil War and Polish emigrants who left their country during the communist period.

Situated between the end of the Second World War and 1991, their digital timeline invites visitors to think about migration through two national histories that are often told separately. It asks what moved people to leave, how political circumstances shaped those departures, and how the memories of those journeys endure within families and communities.

Cold War EuropePolitical exileMigrationFamily historyBelonging
One direction

Greece → Poland

Political refugees left Greece in the aftermath of the Civil War and built lives in Poland. Their displacement links national conflict to experiences of settlement, adaptation and remembrance abroad.

Another direction

Poland → Greece

Polish emigrants left a communist-era Poland for lives elsewhere, including Greece. Their histories add another perspective on political circumstances, mobility and belonging in Cold War Europe.

Curatorial approach

The contributors put these two migrations side by side without suggesting that their causes, scale or lived experiences were identical. The comparison centres their connections while leaving room for historical differences.

02 / Greek political displacement

Greek refugees in Poland

The end of the Greek Civil War was followed by the settlement of Greek political refugees in countries including Poland. This strand of the exhibition draws attention to a history that reaches beyond Greece’s borders and remains important within the memories of families and descendants.

The authors identify R. Witkowski’s research on Greek refugees in Poland after 1949 and Aikaterini Soultania’s doctoral study of the national identity of Greeks expatriated after the Civil War as reference points. Together, these sources situate personal histories within the broader question of how displaced communities sustained and reshaped their identities.

03 / Migration during communism

Polish emigration to Greece

The second strand follows Polish emigrants who left their country during the communist period. Their journeys provide a different vantage point from which to examine how the Cold War affected the possibilities of leaving, resettling and building a life elsewhere.

The submitted source list includes M. Maroufof’s study Polish Immigrants in Greece (2009). Placing this research alongside the history of Greek refugees in Poland creates a conversation about migration across borders and across generations, without reducing either history to a single narrative.

Text guide to the timeline

For readers who cannot access the embedded presentation, the submitted project places two themes in dialogue:

  1. Greek political refugees settling in Poland after the Civil War: their departure, settlement, identity and family memory.
  2. Polish emigrants leaving during the communist period: migration, belonging and the connection between political circumstances and personal decisions.
  3. Shared questions across 1945–1991: how these histories connect Greece and Poland and what they reveal about displacement during the Cold War.

This guide describes the themes confirmed in the final submission. It is not a slide-by-slide transcript of the externally hosted timeline.

05 / Reading across borders

Migration, memory and belonging

The exhibition draws attention to a lesser-known connection in European migration history. By considering two movements in relation to one another, it challenges purely national accounts of migration and encourages visitors to ask whose family histories become part of public memory.

The work also makes a methodological choice: a chronological digital format allows personal and political histories to be read together. The timeline does not replace the source documents or the testimony on which memory depends; it offers one way to arrange these histories so that their connections can be explored.
