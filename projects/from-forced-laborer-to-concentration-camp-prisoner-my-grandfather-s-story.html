The public-history question
Why do some histories stay untold, and what public responsibility do we carry for them? The film approaches this through the forced labourers deported to Germany, whose stories were silenced by shame and marginalization, both in their own societies and in how the Second World War is publicly remembered.
Why this story matters

Because it belongs to millions and is almost never told. The two million Ukrainians deported to Germany as forced labourers were silenced by shame and suspicion, and their memory was lost before it could become public. Recovering one life shows what that silence cost, and why, in a time of renewed war and deportation, these stories still matter.

Sources & Credits

Original project references, visual sources and creator credits are collected here in one place.

  1. Creator & project

    Daria Vystavkina. Short video. Who Owns Memory? Digital Public Exhibition, First Cycle 2026.

  2. Archival records. Prisoner records and the camp-number index obtained on request from the Arolsen Archives (Bad Arolsen), the Auschwitz-Birkenau State Museum, and the Dachau Concentration Camp Memorial. These establish the dates, camps, and prisoner numbers of my grandfather's path through the camp system.

  3. Family archive. Photographs, identity documents on the restoration of his name, and a video recording of my grandfather with his great-grandson.

  4. Press sources. Two Heilbronn newspaper reports on the Kochendorf camp: Angela Groß in Region Heilbronn (2008) and Sandra Weckert in Heilbronner Stimme (2008).

  5. Scholarship. On the Nazi forced-labor system and its scale: Ulrich Herbert, Fremdarbeiter (1985)

  6. Mark Spoerer, Zwangsarbeit unter dem Hakenkreuz (2001)

  7. Spoerer and Fleischhacker, "Forced Laborers in Nazi Germany" (2002). On the Kochendorf salt-mine camp: Klaus Riexinger and Detlef Ernst, Vernichtung durch Arbeit (2003). On Ukrainian forced laborers: Tetiana Pastushenko (Ukrainian Jewish Encounter).
