The Ghana Must Go Bag

When an object outlives its memory

A familiar checked bag carries the name of the 1983 expulsions. What happens when an everyday object survives but the story behind it begins to fade?

By Doris Anderson · Interactive web story

01 · The bag

You know the bag. Do you know the story?

You have probably seen it at a market, a bus station or an airport carousel. It is cheap, light and strong, and it holds more than it seems able to.

Across West Africa, people call it Ghana Must Go. The bag is easy to recognise. The name is harder to explain. Why does an ordinary carrier bag bear the name of one country and an order to leave?

Three ways of reading. Record identifies what historical research documents; testimony presents words from published interviews; interpretation marks the author’s argument. These labels remain distinct throughout this exhibition.

Before reading, did you know where the bag’s name comes from?

02 · The name

A name that came from history

Record. The bag itself has no single home. Historian Samuel Fury Childs Daly describes it as one of the woven plastic bags, often printed in plaid, used across the Global South to carry clothing, produce and trade goods. The name is far more specific: Daly traces it to Nigeria’s expulsion of Ghanaians in 1983. After that deportation, the bags became known as “Ghana-Must-Go”, first in Nigerian Pidgin and then more widely across West Africa. Leicester Museums notes that the name began as a derogatory one.

Interpretation. The object was already ordinary; the expulsion gave it a new name.

03 · 1983

When “go” became a command

Record. Nigeria’s oil economy grew during the 1970s, drawing workers from across the region. As economic conditions worsened, Nigerian politicians blamed Ghanaians for taking jobs. On 17 January 1983, the Minister of Internal Affairs, Alhaji Ali Baba, announced on television that unskilled migrants had to leave the country by the end of the month. Daly notes that the government expected many to comply without direct expulsion.

The scale remains disputed. Ghana’s High Commission estimated about one million Ghanaians were deported; Daly suggests a lower figure, though still in the hundreds of thousands. The event belongs to a longer series of expulsions: Ghana’s Aliens Compliance Order of 1969 and Nigeria’s second, smaller expulsion in May 1985.

04 · Departure

What can a bag carry?

Record. Two weeks is not long to close down a life. The road west from Lagos towards Ghana crossed Benin and Togo. MIDEQ describes checked woven bags among the carriers migrants used for personal belongings.

Lawal also reports that Ghana temporarily closed its border with Togo, stranding travellers in the heat without water. One person’s words cannot stand for everyone. MIDEQ interviews also recorded positive feelings towards Nigerians and attributed the hostility to a state-led anti-migrant narrative.

05 · Object memory

When an object becomes memory

Interpretation. Nothing about the bag’s basic form changed in 1983. The woven plastic, check and zipper remained familiar. What changed was what people called it. Each time someone asks for a “Ghana Must Go”, the command of January 1983 may be spoken again—even when the speaker no longer knows its history.

This is how an everyday object carries collective memory without being made as a monument. In 2023, Migration Advocacy Centre Ghana, MIDEQ researchers and artists marked the fortieth anniversary with an exhibition built around the bag and the documentary Omo Ghana, Man For Chop. In Britain, Anthony Amoako-Attah remade the bag in screen-printed glass as Take Me Home.

“This is a very good programme that is going to cast our minds back to our heritage, what we went through …”Participant at the anniversary exhibition opening, quoted by MIDEQ

06 · Remaking

Reclaimed, or rewritten?

Record. The bag’s check has moved from the market to the studio and runway. Louis Vuitton presented a plaid laundry tote under Marc Jacobs for spring/summer 2007; Aisha Ayensu’s Christie Brown referenced the check in its autumn/winter 2025 collection.

“… it holds the memory of displacement while also celebrating movement, identity, and renewal.”Artist Òbìnna Òbíòmà, quoted in Marie Claire UK

Stylist Marian Kwei describes the pattern as a painful reminder for some; designer Papa Oppong argues that utilitarian objects deserve design innovation on African terms. The bag’s meaning is being reshaped. Whether the history travels with the pattern depends on who is doing the remaking.

07 · Fading

When the object survives the memory

Record. The bag remains familiar in markets and households, as does its name. Yet the history behind the phrase has travelled less widely.

“Few people today connect the bags to the historical event that gave them this name, even though it was not very long ago.”Samuel Fury Childs Daly, 2023

Interpretation. This is the limit of what an object can remember for us. A name can outlast the knowledge of what it once meant.

08 · A question

What does the bag remember?

A bag cannot tell its own story. It can only keep a name ready for someone to ask about.

Who owns this memory? The people who were told to go? The countries that expelled each other’s citizens? The designers who brought the check onto runways? Or the people carrying one home from the market?

The next time you see the bag, say its name—and ask what it meant in January 1983.

Curatorial note

A photographed object is not a photograph of 1983

The two photographs in this exhibition show bags in markets in 2010 and 2013. They do not document the expulsion of 1983. The route and visualisations are explanatory graphics, not contemporaneous photographs. The text distinguishes recorded events, published testimony, and the creator’s interpretation throughout.
