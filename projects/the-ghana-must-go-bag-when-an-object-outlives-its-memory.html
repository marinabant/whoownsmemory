About the project

A short web story about the checked woven plastic bag known across West Africa as "Ghana Must Go". The name comes from Nigeria's expulsion of Ghanaian and other West African migrants in January 1983. The story follows the bag from an ordinary carrier, to a name that records an expulsion, to a pattern now remade by artists and fashion designers. It asks what happens when an object stays familiar while the history behind its name fades. It uses historical scholarship, published testimony, two openly licensed photographs, an interactive timeline and a route map. Live version: https://ghana-must-go-story.netlify.app

Historical setting

The story is set in Nigeria and Ghana, along the coastal road from Lagos through Benin and Togo to Accra. Its central moment is January 1983, within a longer period from Ghana's Aliens Compliance Order of 1969 to Nigeria's second expulsion in 1985, and it follows the bag's later life up to the present. It concerns Ghanaian migrants who lived and worked in Nigeria and were ordered to leave, their memory of that return, and the wider West African public that still uses the bag and its name. The project is transnational.

Why this story matters

Millions of people use this bag and say its name without knowing that the name records a mass expulsion. That makes it a clear case of how memory can survive in everyday language and objects while the knowledge behind it thins out. The story also speaks to the present: migrants are still blamed for economic trouble, and free movement in West Africa is still contested. And as the pattern moves onto runways and into galleries, it raises the question the programme asks: who owns this memory, and who gets to remake it?

The guiding question

How does an everyday object become a carrier of collective memory, and what happens when the object remains recognizable while the history that gave it meaning begins to fade?

ONE YOU KNOW THE BAG. DO YOU KNOW THE STORY?

You have probably seen it at a market, a bus station or an airport carousel. It is cheap, light and strong, and it holds more than it looks able to.

Across West Africa, people call it Ghana Must Go. The bag is easy to recognise. The

name is harder to explain. Why does an ordinary carrier bag bear the name of one

country and an order to leave?

TWO A NAME THAT CAME FROM HISTORY

RECORD The bag itself has no single home. The historian Samuel Fury Childs Daly

describes it as one of the woven plastic bags, often printed in plaid, that people across

the Global South use to carry clothes, produce and trade goods.1

The name is far more specific. Daly traces it to Nigeria’s expulsion of Ghanaians in

1983. After that deportation the bags became known as “Ghana-Must-Go”, first in

Nigerian Pidgin and then across West Africa.1 Leicester Museums, which holds an

artwork based on the bag, notes that the name began as a derogatory one.4

INTERPRETATION The object was already in use and already ordinary. The event gave it a

new name.

THREE 1983: WHEN “GO” BECAME A COMMAND

RECORD Nigeria’s oil economy boomed in the 1970s and drew workers from across the

region. By the end of the decade the boom had turned to bust. Daly writes that Nigerian

politicians deflected attention from their own mismanagement by blaming Ghanaians

for taking Nigerian jobs.1

On 17 January 1983, the Minister of Internal Affairs, Alhaji Ali Baba, announced on

television that unskilled migrants had to leave the country by the end of the month.1

The government expected that most people would comply without being forced. In

Daly’s words, “they would deport themselves.”1

The numbers are disputed. Ghana’s High Commission estimated that about one million

Ghanaians were deported. Daly judges the true figure to be lower, though still in the

hundreds of thousands.1

This was one episode in a longer exchange. Ghana had expelled Nigerians and other

migrants under its Aliens Compliance Order of November 1969, and Nigeria ordered a

second, smaller expulsion in May 1985.1,5

TESTIMONY The journalist Shola Lawal spoke to Ghanaians who made that journey. One

of them, Solomon Asiedu, had been working in Lagos. He told her:

“I was not ready to leave.”

Solomon “Acquaye” Asiedu, quoted by Shola Lawal5

RECORD Lawal also reports that Ghana’s own government closed its border with Togo

for a time, leaving vehicles backed up along the road to Lagos and people stranded in

the heat without water.5

One voice cannot stand for everyone. MIDEQ’s interviews with Ghanaians who lived in

Nigeria in 1983 also recorded positive feelings towards Nigerians, and traced the

expulsion to an anti-migrant narrative led by the state.2

FIVE WHEN AN OBJECT BECOMES MEMORY

INTERPRETATION Nothing about the bag changed in 1983. It was the same woven plastic,

the same check, the same zip. What changed was the word people used for it. Since

then, each time someone asks for a Ghana Must Go in a market, the order of January

1983 is spoken again, whether or not the speaker knows it.

That is how an everyday object comes to carry collective memory. The memory is held

in the name, in daily use, and in the hands of people who never set out to commemorate

anything.

RECORD Some people have chosen to remember on purpose. In 2023, the Migration

Advocacy Centre Ghana, MIDEQ researchers and local artists marked the fortieth

anniversary with an exhibition built around the bags, and with a documentary film, Omo

Ghana, Man For Chop.2,3 One participant said:

“This is a very good programme that is going to cast our minds back to our heritage, what we went through …”

Participant at the exhibition opening, quoted by MIDEQ2

In Britain, the Ghanaian-born artist Anthony Amoako-Attah remade the bag in screen-

printed glass and called it Take Me Home.4

SIX RECLAIMED, OR REWRITTEN?

RECORD The check has also left the market for the studio and the runway. Louis Vuitton

showed a plaid laundry tote under Marc Jacobs for spring/summer 2007.6,7 Aisha

Ayensu, founder of the label Christie Brown, referenced the pattern throughout its

autumn/winter 2025 collection.6

TESTIMONY People who work with the pattern do not describe a wound that has simply

healed. The visual artist Òbìnna Òbíòmà says the print captures a duality:

“… it holds the memory of displacement while also celebrating movement, identity, and renewal.”

Òbìnna Òbíòmà, quoted in Marie Claire UK6

The stylist Marian Kwei says that for some it is a painful reminder of the 1983

expulsion.6 The designer Papa Oppong, quoted in the Cape Times, puts the claim

differently: “We’re asserting that our utilitarian objects are worthy of design innovation

on our own terms.”7

INTERPRETATION The bag’s meaning is being reshaped. A name that began as an insult is

now also worn with pride. Whether that reclaims the story or leaves it behind depends

on who is doing the remaking, and on whether the history travels with the pattern.

SEVEN WHEN THE OBJECT SURVIVES THE MEMORY

RECORD The bag is still everywhere, and so is its name.

The history behind the name has travelled less well. Daly observes:

“Few people today connect the bags to the historical event that gave them this name, even though it was not very long ago.”

Samuel Fury Childs Daly1

INTERPRETATION This is the limit of what an object can remember for us. A name can

outlast the knowledge of what it once meant. For many people who use it now, “Ghana

Must Go” is simply what the bag is called.

EIGHT WHAT DOES THE BAG REMEMBER?

A bag cannot tell its own story. It can only keep a name ready for someone to ask about.

So who owns this memory? The people who were told to go, and who are growing old? The two countries that expelled each other’s citizens? The designers who have put the check on a runway? Or anyone who carries one home from the market?

The next time you see the bag, say its name, and then ask what it meant in January 1983.
