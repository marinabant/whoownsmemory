The Ghana Must Go Bag: When an Object Outlives Its Memory

A familiar woven bag carries the memory of Nigeria’s 1983 expulsion of Ghanaian migrants. This object history traces how an everyday item acquired a name tied to displacement and then travelled into fashion and global culture.

02

You know the bag. Do you know the story?

The checked woven-plastic bag is familiar in markets, bus stations and airports across West Africa. Strong, light and inexpensive, it has acquired the name “Ghana Must Go”. A simple object thus poses a historical question: why does a bag carry a command to leave?

03

A name born of expulsion

The bag long predated its name. Historians trace the expression “Ghana Must Go” to Nigeria’s 1983 expulsion of Ghanaians and other West African migrants. The command became attached to a practical object used by people forced to gather their belongings and depart.

04

1983: when “go” became a command

On 17 January 1983, Nigerian authorities announced that unskilled migrants must leave by the end of the month. Economic crisis and political scapegoating framed the expulsion. Estimates of those affected differ, but the scale of displacement was immense. The event belonged to a longer history of reciprocal expulsion policies, including Ghana’s 1969 Aliens Compliance Order and Nigeria’s further action in 1985.

05

What can a bag carry?

Forced migrants faced difficult journeys through Benin and Togo towards Ghana, taking only what they could pack and carry. A woven bag might contain clothing and household possessions, but it could not hold a lost home, abandoned relationships or an interrupted livelihood.

06

From an object to a memory

The phrase outlived the political episode. Bags of the same kind circulated across borders, and the name became part of the vocabulary of migration. The story distinguishes what historical sources document from people’s later uses and interpretations of the object.

07

Remade and reimagined

Versions of the bag entered fashion, art and everyday commerce. Such reinterpretations can make an object newly visible while separating it from the violence that gave it a name. The question is not whether an object may change, but whether its history travels with it.

08

When memory fades

Many people recognise the patterned bag without knowing why it is called “Ghana Must Go”. The distance between name and event reveals how readily public memory can survive as a phrase after its origins become obscure.

09

What the bag remembers

Remembering requires returning the history of expulsion to the object. The exhibition invites visitors to ask how language preserves hostility, how ordinary goods carry collective memory, and what responsibilities accompany reusing names born in displacement.

10

Places, memory and interpretation

The story is set in Nigeria and Ghana, along the coastal road from Lagos through Benin and Togo to Accra. Its central moment is January 1983, within a longer period from Ghana's Aliens Compliance Order of 1969 to Nigeria's second expulsion in 1985, and it follows the bag's later life up to the present. It concerns Ghanaian migrants who lived and worked in Nigeria and were ordered to leave, their memory of that return, and the wider West African public that still uses the bag and its name. The project is transnational.

Public-history question

How can an everyday object carry the history of an expulsion?
