The story

Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage: Sites of Memory presents the Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage Audio Archive as a translocal and community-led public-history project concerned with the contested memory of the eighteenth-century African philosopher Anton Wilhelm Amo. Rather than reconstructing Amo through a single authoritative biography, the project connects places, voices, archival traces, images, audio recordings and contemporary memory practices across Germany. It brings academic research into conversation with community knowledge, local memory work, artitistic practise and the perspectives of people and initiatives who have shaped how Amo is remembered today. The archive can be understood as a moving archive: knowledge about Amo does not remain in one institution, collection or national history, but circulates between places, communities and generations. Like a kind of weather map of memory, the project makes visible where memories accumulate, where narratives collide, where silences remain and where new forms of remembrance emerge. The exhibition contribution is adapted from my scholarly work “Mapping Amo, Hearing Place: The Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage Audio Archive as Black Cartography and Locative Sound Art” (DOI: 10.11588/artdok.00010180).

Where and when?

The story is both transnational and digital. Its historical point of departure is the life and afterlives of Anton Wilhelm Amo, who lived and worked between West Africa and eighteenth-century Europe. The project follows the ways in which his biography and memory have subsequently been produced, preserved, forgotten, rediscovered and contested across different historical periods. Geographically, the archive connects sites of memory in Germany – including Berlin, Halle, Wittenberg, Jena and other locations connected to Amo’s intellectual and memorial history – with places and knowledge networks in Ghana and the Netherlands. The contemporary story concerns a wide range of actors: Black and African-diasporic communities, local memory initiatives, researchers, activists, cultural institutions, educators, descendants and local knowledge holders, as well as the Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage Society e.V. The project grew from long-term research and community-based memory work and asks how a history dispersed across archives, cities, countries and communities can be made publicly accessible without collapsing those different perspectives into one authoritative narrative.

Central public-history question

Who has the authority to map, preserve and narrate Anton Wilhelm Amo’s memory – and what changes when archives, communities, places and institutions are allowed to speak alongside rather than through one another?

Why does this story matter?

Anton Wilhelm Amo is increasingly visible in European public memory, yet the knowledge through which his life is reconstructed remains fragmented across countries, archives, institutions, scholarly traditions and community histories. This matters because public history is never only about recovering information from the past. It is also about deciding which sources count, whose knowledge becomes visible, who is authorised to interpret historical absence, and where memory is allowed to reside. The Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage Audio Archive responds to this problem by treating place, sound and community knowledge as historical sources in their own right. Rather than presenting one final version of Amo’s biography, the archive makes disagreement and fragmentation visible. Different interpretations, absences and memory traditions can coexist. Accessibility is therefore not only a technical question within the project. It is also epistemic and geographical: how can people gain access to knowledge that is dispersed between institutions, countries and communities? How can public history make visible voices and forms of knowledge that have often remained outside conventional archives? The project is freely accessible, bilingual in German and English, multimodal and designed to be experienced both at physical sites of memory and remotely. Audio, text, images, maps, transcripts where available, captions and source material provide different routes into the archive. In this sense, the project does not simply digitise an existing archive. It asks what an archive can become when memory is understood as mobile, contested and collectively produced.

Sources & evidence

The project draws on a combination of: archival records and historical documents relating to Anton Wilhelm Amo and the institutions and places connected to his life and reception; Amo’s surviving published writings and historical university records; scholarly research on Amo, African-European intellectual history, colonial history and memory; oral-history interviews and recorded conversations conducted in the context of long-term research and community memory work; photographs, maps and documentation of sites of memory; materials produced by civil-society initiatives and local remembrance projects; contemporary interviews, audio recordings and site-based documentation created for the Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage Audio Archive; community knowledge and locally situated forms of historical remembrance in Germany and Ghana. The exhibition contribution is conceptually based on my scholarly publication: Andrea-Vicky Amankwaa-Birago, “Mapping Amo, Hearing Place: The Anton Wilhelm Amo Heritage Audio Archive as Black Cartography and Locative Sound Art.” DOI: 10.11588/artdok.00010180.
