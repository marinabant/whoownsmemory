An introduction

Jewish presence within a changing city

This digital public-history project traces the historical presence of the Jewish community of Ioannina through selected places in the city.

Focusing on Jewish neighbourhoods, religious and educational sites, the deportation of 1944, and the traces that remain in the present-day urban landscape, the project explores how Jewish presence, destruction, absence and memory can be read through place. An interactive map connects surviving, lost and transformed sites with the contemporary city.

How can we read the traces of a community in a city where much of its physical presence has disappeared or changed?

01 / Origins and settlement

A Community within the City

The Jewish presence in Ioannina can be documented at least as far back as the early fourteenth century. A chrysobull issued by the Byzantine emperor Andronikos II Palaiologos in 1319 explicitly refers to the “Jews of the city”, providing evidence of a Jewish community in Ioannina at that time.1

Over the following centuries, Jewish settlement developed both within the Castle and beyond its walls, including the Sarava and Tsoukala neighbourhoods and areas around the city’s commercial centre.2 Today, much of this historical geography is difficult to recognise.

By tracing selected sites that have survived, disappeared or changed, this project explores how Jewish presence and absence remain embedded in the contemporary city.

02 / The fabric of everyday life

The Jewish Neighborhoods: Spaces of Everyday Life

The Jewish neighbourhoods were spaces where domestic, religious and economic life intersected. Jewish residents lived around the Castle and in neighbourhoods connected with the commercial centre, while Romaniote Jews participated in occupations ranging from small-scale trade to larger mercantile activities, including the textile and silk trades.3

Their everyday life also reflected close interaction with the wider cultural environment of Ioannina. The Romaniote Jews were Greek-speaking, and elements of local Epirote culture became part of their distinctive communal traditions.4

The Jewish neighbourhoods were therefore identifiable spaces of communal life while remaining closely connected to the wider social and economic landscape of the city.

03 / Worship, learning and belonging

Synagogue, School and Community Life

Religious life was closely connected to the urban presence of the Jewish community of Ioannina. The Old Synagogue, Kahal Kadosh Yashan, was a place of worship for the city’s Jewish community.5

At the beginning of the twentieth century, Jewish education in Ioannina was increasingly connected with the Alliance Israélite Universelle. The Alliance school stood next to the New Synagogue (Kahal Kadosh Hadash), in the Jewish neighbourhood of Megali Rouga, along present-day Yosef Eliya Street.6

These institutions formed important spaces of communal life, linking religious tradition with new forms of education and embedding both worship and learning within the Jewish geography of the city.

04 / The rupture

March 1944: The Rupture

Content note

This chapter describes Nazi persecution and the deportation of Ioannina’s Jewish community. The historical photograph below documents the roundup of civilians on 25 March 1944, including children.

On 25 March 1944, the centuries-long Jewish presence in Ioannina was violently disrupted. German forces, with the cooperation of the Greek Gendarmerie, carried out an operation to surround the areas inhabited by the city’s Jewish population and assemble them for deportation.7

Jews were gathered in the lakeside area of Mavili Square, where families were separated and the Jewish population was loaded onto German military trucks. They were transported out of Ioannina and subsequently deported to Auschwitz-Birkenau.8

The deportation devastated the Jewish community and radically altered the human geography of Ioannina, leaving behind neighbourhoods, buildings and places from which much of the community that had given them meaning was now absent.

05 / Survival, loss and transformation

What Remains? Reading Jewish History in the Present-Day City

The destruction of the Jewish community in 1944 profoundly changed the human landscape of Ioannina, but it did not erase every trace of its presence. Today, the Old Synagogue, Kahal Kadosh Yashan, still stands inside the Castle and remains an active place of worship and memory.

The Jewish cemetery is also maintained by the community, while several houses associated with Jewish residents survive within the modern city.9 Other important sites can no longer be encountered in their original form. The New Synagogue and the Alliance Boys’ School were destroyed, and apartment buildings belonging to the Jewish Community now stand on their former sites.10

The interactive map brings together the surviving, lost and transformed sites discussed in this project, alongside the approximate spatial boundaries of the historical Jewish neighbourhoods of Ioannina. It allows the historical geography of the community to be read in relation to the present-day urban landscape.

These different forms of survival, disappearance and transformation make the contemporary city itself a historical source. A visitor moving through Ioannina may encounter a surviving synagogue, a cemetery, an ordinary apartment building or a public square without immediately recognising the histories connected to them.

Mavili Square is particularly significant in this respect: today an open public space beside the lake, it was one of the places where the Jewish population was assembled for deportation on 25 March 1944.

Mapping these locations therefore does more than reconstruct the former geography of a Jewish community. It brings different layers of the city into relation with one another, connecting places of everyday life, worship and education with sites of persecution and with the transformed urban landscape of the present.

The history of Jewish Ioannina can thus be read not only through what has survived, but also through what has disappeared or changed. The map makes these absences visible, allowing the contemporary city to be read alongside the community that once inhabited it.

Curatorial note

This exhibition combines the author’s historical narrative with an interactive map. Locations labelled as lost or transformed document changes to the urban fabric; the map’s historical neighbourhood boundaries are approximate. The surviving synagogue, cemetery and streets remain part of a living city and, in some cases, an active Jewish community. Mapping their histories does not imply that Jewish life in Ioannina ended in 1944.
