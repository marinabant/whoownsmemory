Mapping a Lost Community

Jewish Life, Absence and Memory in Ioannina

This digital public history project traces the historical presence of the Jewish community of Ioannina through selected places in the city

The public-history question
How can we read the traces of a community in a city where much of its physical presence has disappeared or changed?
Why this story matters

This story matters because much of the historic Jewish presence in Ioannina is no longer immediately visible in the contemporary city. By connecting surviving places with sites that have disappeared or been transformed, the project makes this history more accessible to present-day residents and visitors. It also shows how urban space can preserve traces of both everyday communal life and historical rupture, encouraging reflection on how absence itself can become part of public memory.

CHAPTER 01

A community within the city

Mapping a lost community: Tracing Jewish Life, Absence and Memory in the Urban Landscape of

The Jewish presence in Ioannina can be documented at least as far back as the early fourteenth century. A chrysobull issued by the Byzantine emperor Andronikos II Palaiologos in 1319 explicitly refers to the “Jews of the city”, providing evidence of a Jewish community in Ioannina at that time.1 Over the following centuries, Jewish settlement developed both within the Castle and beyond its walls, including the Sarava and Tsoukala neighborhoods and areas around the city’s commercial center.2 Today, much of this historical geography is difficult to recognize. By tracing selected sites that have survived, disappeared or changed, this project explores how Jewish presence and absence remain embedded in the contemporary city.

The Jewish Neighborhoods: Spaces of Everyday Life

The Jewish neighborhoods were spaces where domestic, religious and economic life intersected. Jewish residents lived around the Castle and in neighborhoods connected with the commercial center, while Romaniote Jews participated in occupations ranging from small-scale trade to larger mercantile activities, including the textile and silk trades.3 Their everyday life also reflected close interaction with the wider cultural environment of Ioannina. The Romaniote Jews were Greek-speaking and elements of local Epirote culture became part of their distinctive communal traditions.4 The Jewish neighborhoods were therefore identifiable spaces of communal life while remaining closely connected to the wider social and economic landscape of the city.

CHAPTER 02

Synagogues and institutions

Synagogue, School and Community Life

Religious life was closely connected to the urban presence of the Jewish community of Ioannina. The Old Synagogue, Kahal Kadosh Yashan, was a place of worship for the city’s Jewish community.5 At the beginning of the twentieth century, Jewish education in Ioannina was increasingly connected with the Alliance Israélite Universelle. The Alliance school stood next to the New Synagogue (Kahal Kadosh Hadash), in the Jewish neighborhood of Megali Rouga, along present-day Yosef Eliya Street.6 These institutions formed important spaces of communal life, linking religious tradition with new forms of education and embedding both worship and learning within the Jewish geography of the city.

On 25 March 1944, the centuries-long Jewish presence in Ioannina was violently disrupted. German forces, with the cooperation of the Greek Gendarmerie, carried out an operation to surround the areas inhabited by the city’s Jewish population and assemble them for deportation.7 Jews were gathered in the lakeside area of Mavili Square, where families were separated and the Jewish population was loaded onto German military trucks. They were transported out of Ioannina and subsequently deported to Auschwitz- Birkenau.8

CHAPTER 03

March 1944: deportation

The deportation devastated the Jewish community and radically altered the human geography of Ioannina, leaving behind neighborhoods, buildings and places from which much of the community that had given them meaning was now absent.

What Remains? Reading Jewish History in the Present-Day City

The destruction of the Jewish community in 1944 profoundly changed the human landscape of Ioannina, but it did not erase every trace of its presence. Today, the Old Synagogue, Kahal Kadosh Yashan, still stands inside the Castle and remains an active place of worship and memory. The Jewish cemetery is also maintained by the community, while several houses associated with Jewish residents survive within the modern city.9 Other important sites can no longer be encountered in their original form. The New Synagogue and the Alliance Boys’ School were destroyed and apartment buildings belonging to the Jewish Community now stand on their former sites.10

CHAPTER 04

Mapping traces and absences

The interactive map brings together the surviving, lost and transformed sites discussed in this project, alongside the approximate spatial boundaries of the historical Jewish neighborhoods of Ioannina. It allows the historical geography of the community to be read in relation to the present-day urban landscape.

Explore the interactive map: Mapping a Lost Community: Jewish Ioannina

These different forms of survival, disappearance and transformation make the contemporary city itself a historical source. A visitor moving through Ioannina may encounter a surviving synagogue, a cemetery, an ordinary apartment building or a public square without immediately recognizing the histories connected to them. Mavili Square is particularly significant in this respect: today an open public space beside the lake, it was one of the places where the Jewish population was assembled for deportation on 25 March 1944.

Mapping these locations therefore does more than reconstruct the former geography of a Jewish community. It brings different lafters of the city into relation with one another, connecting places of everyday life, worship and education with sites of persecution and with the transformed urban landscape of the present. The history of Jewish Ioannina can thus be read not only through what has survived, but also through what has disappeared or changed. The map makes these absences visible, allowing the contemporary city to be read alongside the community that once inhabited it.
