Migration & Belonging

Kneading Memories: Delhi’s post-Partition Rotiscape

Meher Nandrajog•Social-media storytelling project•Solo

The story

My story is situated in contemporary Delhi and concerns the culinary memories the city preserves in the present. I have tried to examine of varieties of rotis in Delhi (India's capital city, with a long migrant history) as archives of migration and change, with a focus on 1947 Partition and its afterlives. Focusing on rotis across the city and its (Punjabi) refugee colonies and neighbourhoods, I wish to enquire how culinary practices encode histories of displacement, continuity, adaptation, and rebuilding homes, and wish to explore how various rotis carry memories of lost/new homes, reconfigured kitchens, and evolving identities. My ongoing research is displayed on the Instagram and Substack pages I have made for this project.

Where and when?

My story is situated in contemporary Delhi and its Punjabi refugee colonies, neighbourhoods, and homes, in the context of 1947 Partition. My ongoing research is displayed digitally on the Instagram and Substack pages I have made for this project.

Central public-history question

The central question around which I hope to work is: How does the evolution of rotis in Delhi register its layered history of migration, especially after the Partition of the subcontinent in 1947?

Why does this story matter?

Given the context of divisive contemporary cultures, it may be useful to remember the multi-cultural, assimilative history of food practices in the space of Delhi, a politically important site as the capital city of India, and as a multi-refugee space. I hope to see this through the varieties of rotis that Delhi has to offer.

Sources & evidence

Every picture used (in the background or foreground) has been personally clicked, with all required consent and permissions wherever applicable and necessary. All my information has been gathered from a variety of sources that are research materials/writings/talks by researchers/scholars/cultural historians who I have encountered over time and across multiple platforms. It is, however, substantially based on informal ‘oral histories’ narrated to me, as well as my own lived experiences as part of the third generation of people who belong to such a history, yet are distanced from it.

Public-history lens

This project is presented within Migration, Displacement, Minorities & Belonging. Read it not only for what happened in the past, but for how sources, memory, place, visibility and interpretation shape what becomes public history.
