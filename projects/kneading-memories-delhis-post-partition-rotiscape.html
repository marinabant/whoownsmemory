01 / The public-history question

What can a roti remember?

This story begins with the culinary memories that contemporary Delhi preserves in its everyday food practices.

Meher Nandrajog approaches varieties of roti—forms of flatbread prepared across the city—as archives of migration and change, with particular attention to the Partition of the Indian subcontinent in 1947 and its continuing legacies.

How does the evolution of rotis in Delhi register its layered history of migration, especially after the Partition of the subcontinent in 1947?Meher Nandrajog · Central public-history question

02 / Historical setting

Delhi, a city of arrivals

Located in contemporary Delhi, this project examines the city’s long history of migration through the places where food is prepared, shared, and remembered. The focus is on Punjabi refugee colonies, other neighbourhoods, and homes shaped by the 1947 Partition.

For the project, the city is a place where histories of movement are not confined to formal memorials. They can also be considered through practices that have become part of ordinary domestic life.

03 / Food and place

Lost homes, new kitchens

Looking at rotis across Delhi and its Punjabi refugee neighbourhoods, Nandrajog asks how culinary practices carry histories of displacement, continuity, adaptation and the rebuilding of homes. Different breads become an entry point into questions about lost and newly established homes, reconfigured kitchens, and evolving identities.

The inquiry is concerned not only with the survival of recipes but with change: how a familiar food practice may acquire new meanings as families move and establish themselves elsewhere.

04 / Research and testimony

Listening across generations

Much of this research is grounded in informal oral histories narrated to Nandrajog. Her account also draws on her own lived experience as a third-generation descendant of a history to which she belongs, even while feeling a degree of distance from it.

Alongside these conversations and experiences, the project draws on materials, writing and talks by researchers, scholars and cultural historians encountered across different platforms.

This combination of family recollection, lived experience and wider scholarship makes the domestic sphere a meaningful place from which to ask historical questions. The project continues through social-media storytelling, with research shared on Instagram and Substack.

About the oral histories

The final submission describes informal oral histories but does not provide individual transcripts, named interviewees or publication-ready recordings. None are invented or presented here as verbatim testimony.

05 / Why the story matters

A shared food history in a divided present

For Nandrajog, remembering the multicultural and assimilative history of food in Delhi has particular significance amid contemporary social divisions. India’s capital has also been a space of refuge and resettlement, and its culinary practices provide a way to consider the layered histories of people who have made lives there.

By attending to varieties of roti, the project asks visitors to look at familiar food through the lens of displacement, adaptation and shared belonging. The everyday practice of making bread becomes a starting point for public-history inquiry.

To consider a food practice as an archive is to ask not only what has been preserved, but also what has changed as people have moved and made homes again.

Curatorial note

This exhibition presents *Kneading Memories* as an ongoing inquiry rather than a completed catalogue of Delhi’s breads or a reconstruction of any single family’s journey. The text is based on the contributor’s final project submission and has been lightly copyedited and organised for online reading. Interpretive transitions and section headings are editorial.

The flatbread illustration above was created for the layout. It is not historical evidence.
