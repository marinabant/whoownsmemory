Method

How we do digital public history

Digital public history is a public-facing historical practice shaped by questions, evidence, interpretation, participation, ethics and form.

Story first. Tool second.

The project’s organising rule is simple: begin with a focused question, audience, evidence and ethical plan. Choose technology only after the historical purpose is clear.

Choose a thematic track

Begin with a broad field of memory: difficult pasts, migration, place, testimony, archives, digital memory or another connected area.

Find the smallest meaningful centre

Centre the story on one person, place, object, photograph, testimony, event, document or digital claim.

Write the public-history question

Use a question that connects the historical case to visibility, memory, responsibility or participation.

Define a real audience

Local residents, learners, descendants, museum visitors, teachers or another specific public — not simply “everyone”.

Establish public significance

Explain why the story matters now without forcing the past into a present-day slogan.

Build the source base

Record provenance, creator, date, repository, reliability, limits, rights and citation details.

Identify missing or vulnerable perspectives

Ask whose voice is absent, mediated, over-exposed or spoken for. Do not invent a voice.

Complete ethics & rights review

Resolve consent, privacy, copyright, anonymity, community sensitivity and possible harm before publication.

Design the narrative arc

Move from hook and orientation through evidence, complexity and interpretation to a closing question or reflection.

Choose the simplest effective digital form

Story first, tool second: web story, map, timeline, audio, video, photo essay or another form that serves the evidence.

Prototype early

Test readability, media use, accessibility and technical feasibility before polishing.

Review, revise and publish

Check factual accuracy, source transparency, rights, accessibility, public clarity, design and technical function.

Reading sources

Sources are evidence, not illustrations

Photographs, oral testimony, official records, family objects, maps and digital traces answer different questions. They must be contextualised, compared and interpreted.

Provenance

Who created the source, when, where, for what purpose, and how did it survive?

Perspective

Whose voice or viewpoint is present? Whose is mediated, absent or structurally excluded?

Context

What historical circumstances shaped what could be recorded, said, photographed or preserved?

Limits

What can the source help us know — and what can it not tell us?

Rights & care

Can the source be reused lawfully and responsibly? What are the implications for living people and communities?

Interpretation

Distinguish the source itself from the claims and meanings historians construct from it.
